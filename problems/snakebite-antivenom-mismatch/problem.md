# Snakebite: the cheap cure exists, but half the antivenom in circulation can't neutralize the local snake

> Intake brief from the scout routine, 2026-06-26. Category: global health and disease (shape pick; rotation lap complete).
> Bottleneck type: misaligned incentives (the right antivenom is too unprofitable to make, the wrong one is cheap and aggressively marketed) compounded by a synthesis grind nobody is staffed to do — matching venom geography to where each product is actually stocked.

## The problem, in plain language

Snakebite envenoming kills an estimated 81,000–138,000 people a year and leaves roughly three times as many with amputations or permanent disability, overwhelmingly poor rural farmers in South Asia and sub-Saharan Africa ([WHO](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/snakebite-envenoming)). India alone accounts for ~50,000 deaths annually ([Nature Communications 2022](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-022-33627-9)). Unlike most neglected tropical diseases, there is a cure that has existed for over a century: antivenom. The horror is that much of the antivenom physically present in clinics does not work against the snakes biting people nearby.

## The half-known solution

Antivenom raised against venom from one snake population often fails against the same species elsewhere. Indian polyvalent antivenom is made from venom milked in a few southern districts; it shows poor preclinical neutralization of Russell's viper, cobra, and saw-scaled viper populations from other Indian biogeographic zones — including near-total failure against desert cobra venom ([PMC7924803](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7924803/); [PMC7993602](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7993602/)). The "Big Four" framing means medically important species outside it are not covered at all ([Oxford/TRSTMH 2025](https://academic.oup.com/trstmh/article/119/9/1016/8113135)). In Africa, the highly effective Fav-Afrique was discontinued by Sanofi in 2014 on profitability grounds; the continent is now "awash" in cheaper Indian-made antivenoms marketed for species they were never validated against ([MSF Access](https://msfaccess.org/how-sanofi-slithered-its-way-out-neglected-antivenom-market); [The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/unsuitable-antivenoms-are-being-sold-in-africa-costing-lives-83658)). The fix is not new science: it is matching the right validated product to the right geography, and flagging the mismatches.

## Why it's stuck

The right antivenom is unprofitable (a treatment course costs ~$13–110 in product but markets are poor and fragmented), while the wrong antivenom is cheap and sells ([PMC4287484](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4287484/)). The evidence proving mismatch is scattered across dozens of venom-proteomics and preclinical-neutralization papers, each covering one species in one region, never assembled into a procurement-grade map. Regulators register antivenoms nationally without efficacy data tied to local venom. Peripheral health centres often refer rather than treat, unsure of dosing or whether their stock even works ([Lancet SEA 2025](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lansea/article/PIIS2772-3682(25)00069-1/fulltext)).

## The AI-agent desk-research wedge

A model running unsupervised for hours can build the artifact nobody is staffed to build: a structured, district-level matrix cross-referencing (a) which snake species and venom variants dominate each region, (b) which antivenoms are registered/stocked there, and (c) the published preclinical neutralization evidence for that product against that variant — surfacing the cells where the stocked product is documented to fail or is untested. It can compile the post-Fav-Afrique African product registry against WHO's own assessment data, flag products marketed beyond their validated species, and produce per-country "your shelf vs. your snakes" briefs. This is literature synthesis and structured extraction, not new lab work — directly handable to a procurement officer or regulator.

## Who is closest today

Wellcome's £80M/7-year snakebite program funds market revival and next-generation (recombinant/monoclonal) treatments — invention and supply, not the deployment-mismatch audit ([Wellcome](https://wellcome.org/what-we-do/our-work/snakebites); [Science 2019](https://www.science.org/content/article/snakebites-globally-neglected-killer-get-transformational-injection-research-funds)). India's NAPSE (launched March 2024) aims to halve deaths by 2030 but lacks a venom-to-stock efficacy map ([PMC12240341](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12240341/)). WHO maintains an antivenom assessment process and regional venom data ([WHO](https://www.who.int/activities/addressing-antivenoms-issues)). Academic venom-proteomics groups (e.g. in India and at the Liverpool School of Tropical Medicine) publish the per-species evidence but none has synthesized it into an actionable procurement map. That synthesis is the wedge.
