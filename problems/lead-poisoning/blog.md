# The lead map nobody has drawn

Roughly 800 million children, one in three on Earth, carry enough lead in their blood to permanently lose IQ, attention, and earning power. The Global Burden of Disease attributes about 1.5 million deaths a year to lead; a 2023 Lancet Planetary Health model puts the cardiovascular toll near 5.5 million. In 2019 alone, lead cost an estimated 765 million IQ points and $1.4 trillion in lost lifetime income, up to roughly 8% of GDP in the poorest countries. Lead produces no outbreak and no acute ward, so it gets no constituency.

## Why it is stuck

The fix is not a vaccine. It is finding which source poisons children in each country and switching it off. The dominant source differs by country: paint, turmeric dyed with lead chromate, informal battery recycling, glazed cookware, kohl cosmetics. And here is the bottleneck, exactly: of about 137 low- and middle-income countries, pooled child blood-lead measurements exist for only 34 (Ericson et al., Lancet Planetary Health, 2021). Everywhere else the burden number is a model, and a model cannot tell a regulator whether to raid the paint factory or the spice mill.

Money makes it worse. Global funding ran near $15 million a year until 2024. The $150 million USAID/UNICEF Partnership for a Lead-Free Future was gutted in early 2025 when USAID was dismantled. What survived is philanthropy: Open Philanthropy's $104 million Lead Exposure Action Fund (LEAF), which must allocate its money by end of 2027.

## Why now

The precedent exists and it is cheap. In Bangladesh, Stanford and icddr,b researchers traced child exposure to turmeric adulterated with lead chromate, then paired media pressure, face-to-face work with mill owners, and handheld XRF enforcement by the food-safety authority. Market turmeric with detectable lead fell from 47% in 2019 to 0% in 2021. Mill workers' blood lead dropped about 30%. Cost: roughly $1 per DALY averted, among the best buys in global health.

Measurement is also starting to move. Bhutan ran its first national child blood-lead survey in 2024 (about 3,000 children) and found 75.9% of children aged 1 to 6 above 3.5 µg/dL. UNICEF is rolling blood-lead modules into MICS surveys, starting with Bangladesh. The question is where the next field team and the next marginal dollar should go. This project ran that question through four desk-research stages: a data inventory across 25 high-burden countries, a scored 10-country shortlist, source-attribution profiles for the top 3, and a regulator-ready dossier for the winner.

## What the research found

The naive expectation is that poor countries lack laws and the worst-burden giants are the best targets. Both turned out wrong.

**The best target already has the law.** Indonesia ranked first on the shortlist (3.60 on a weighted 1-to-5 rubric of burden, data gap, tractability, and whitespace; Egypt 3.50, Philippines 3.40). Indonesia has classified spent lead-acid batteries as hazardous waste since at least 2014 and bans unlicensed smelting under Government Regulation 22/2021 and Ministry of Environment Regulation 6/2021. Enforcement is the gap: the ministry has issued about 5 smelting permits while Pure Earth estimates more than 200 illegal smelting sites operate, 34 in greater Jakarta alone. In a 2019 study of three Greater Jakarta neighborhoods with informal battery recycling, 47% of children aged 1 to 5 had blood lead at or above 5 µg/dL. The ministry's own hazardous-waste director-general told National Geographic that authorities "can't close" the smelters: operators would lose their livelihoods and relocate to hidden sites. A civil-society group first counted 71 illegal smelters around 2008. Twenty years of a law without a map.

**Greenfield is gone.** Pure Earth's LEAF-funded program, started in late 2024, already covers Colombia, Egypt, Ghana, India, Indonesia, Peru, and the Philippines: seven of the most tractable high-burden countries. The highest-burden giants (India, Nigeria, Pakistan, all scoring 5 of 5 on burden) landed in the bottom half of the shortlist because they are both hard to operate in and already covered by every major program. The marginal opportunity is not entering new countries. It is deepening: linking measured sources to measured blood.

**Each country knows exactly half the problem.** The Philippines has shelf data and no blood data: a 2021-22 market survey of 856 samples found 33% of cosmetics, 24% of metal cookware, 16% of paints, and 13% of ceramic foodware above lead thresholds, but there is no recent national child blood-lead survey to say which one poisons children. Indonesia is the inverse: blood data near smelters, no country-level product data. Egypt has neither; its best evidence is one decade-old Cairo study (pottery-workshop children averaged 43.3 µg/dL), it has no lead limit for ceramics at all, and its 2022 paint decree still permits 5,000 ppm of lead in colored paints. And five high-burden countries (DRC, Afghanistan, Yemen, Myanmar, Madagascar) have no usable blood-lead survey of any kind.

## What it takes

The funder brief prices three core purchases, anchored to real budgets: Pure Earth's Rapid Market Screening cost about $36,800 per country ($920,000 across 25 countries, per GiveWell's grant analysis), and Bhutan's national survey required about $215,000 of donated lab equipment.

First, a georeferenced contamination-plus-blood map of Jakarta's informal smelter clusters, paired child blood sampling in smelter and non-smelter areas: roughly $150,000 to $350,000. This is the evidence that makes enforcement politically survivable. Second, a formal-sector off-ramp pilot routing informal collectors to licensed smelters before any closures, about $0.5 million to $2 million. This step has no Bangladesh analog and is the most important adaptation: turmeric mills could stop adding pigment at near-zero cost, but smelting is the operator's entire income, so enforcement without an off-ramp produces displacement, not elimination. Third, market screening plus a first child blood-lead baseline in Egypt and the Philippines, roughly $175,000 to $400,000 combined. Total core package: $0.9 million to $2.9 million, a small slice of LEAF's $104 million envelope. Pure Earth (already in all three countries), the EcoWaste Coalition in the Philippines, KPBB and university partners in Indonesia could execute; the Indonesian environment ministry owns enforcement.

## Open questions

Desk research cannot resolve the central one: whether battery recycling, severe near smelters, drives Indonesia's modeled 7.89 million elevated-lead children, or whether ubiquitous cookware and ceramics carry the national burden. The Jakarta blood study is a single cross-sectional sample from 2019. The smelter counts come from Pure Earth estimates and journalism, not audited registries. The country ranking weights tractability at 30%, a value judgment that pushes India and Nigeria down; a funder optimizing for raw children affected would rank differently. And money cannot buy the two real constraints: trained field teams who can run XRF guns and draw blood, and a regulator's willingness to act. The map and the off-ramp lower the political cost of enforcement. The decision stays in Jakarta.

What is no longer in doubt is the shape of the problem. The science is settled, the fixes are cheap, the precedent removed a national exposure source for about $1 per DALY, and the next target has had its law on the books for over a decade. What is missing is a map that costs less than a house in the city it would cover.
