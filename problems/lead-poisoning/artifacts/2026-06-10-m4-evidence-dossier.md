## 1. Executive summary (≤1 page)

Indonesia has the largest *modeled* child-lead burden of any country in this project's tractable shortlist — IHME GBD 2021 estimates roughly **7.89 million Indonesian children (≈7.1%) with blood lead level (BLL) >5 µg/dL** [IHME 2021, reported by Pure Earth — https://www.pureearth.org/mitigating-lead-exposure-in-low-and-middle-income-countries/; M1-B6]. Among candidate exposure sources, only one carries direct, locally measured, source-linked blood-lead evidence: **informal recycling of used lead-acid batteries (ULAB)**. In a cross-sectional study of children aged 1–5 in three Greater Jakarta (Jabodetabek) neighborhoods with informal ULAB recycling, **47% of children had BLL ≥5 µg/dL and 9% ≥10 µg/dL** [Suprapto et al., *Int J Environ Res Public Health* 2019;16(7):1276 — https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6480953/; M3 Country 1]. Pure Earth estimates **more than 200 illegal ULAB smelting locations** against only about **five licensed** smelters [Pure Earth Indonesia ULAB project — https://www.pureearth.org/project/mitigating-lead-exposure-in-indonesia-used-lead-acid-batteries/; National Geographic — https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/indonesia-s-toxic-toll].

This is **not a missing-law problem; it is a non-enforcement problem.** Indonesia already classifies spent lead-acid batteries as hazardous waste (limbah B3) and prohibits their unlicensed processing under **Government Regulation (PP) No. 22 of 2021** (which superseded PP 101/2014) and **Minister of Environment and Forestry Regulation No. 6 of 2021** on B3 waste technical procedures, both enforced by **KLHK** [SUPRA International, B3 status review — https://supra-international.com/insights/indonesia-s-b3-waste-management-current-status-and-challenges-in-hazardous-waste-regulation-and-implementation; Enviliance ASIA — https://enviliance.com/regions/southeast-asia/id/report_5056]. KLHK's own director-general for hazardous waste has publicly acknowledged the illegal smelters but stated authorities "can't close" them because too many people would lose jobs and operators would relocate to hidden sites [National Geographic — https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/indonesia-s-toxic-toll]. The binding constraint is therefore political-economic and enforcement-capacity, not statutory.

The Bangladesh turmeric case showed that a tightly-sequenced combination of (i) credible local measurement, (ii) media amplification, (iii) face-to-face engagement with the responsible businesses, (iv) handheld-XRF-enabled enforcement of an *already-existing* prohibition, and (v) follow-up monitoring can collapse a contamination source — turmeric samples with detectable lead fell from **47% (2019) to 0% (2021)** and children's median BLL fell ~30% [Forsyth et al. / Bangladesh enforcement study, *Environ Res* 2023 — https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0013935123011325; undark — https://undark.org/2023/07/19/the-vice-of-spice-confronting-lead-tainted-turmeric/]. The same five-stage logic can be adapted to Indonesian ULAB — but with a crucial difference: turmeric adulteration was a *cheap-to-substitute* practice (mills could simply stop adding pigment), whereas informal smelting is a *livelihood and a price arbitrage*. The dossier below adapts each playbook step to that harder reality and flags where a literal copy would fail.

**Bottom line for the regulator:** the legal authority to act already exists; what is missing is (a) a defensible, georeferenced contamination map paired with child-BLL data to make enforcement politically survivable, (b) a formal-sector off-ramp so that closing informal smelters does not simply displace them, and (c) sustained handheld-XRF-based site monitoring. **Field XRF should point first at informal ULAB smelter villages in the Jabodetabek industrial fringe** [M3 Country 1].

## 2. The contamination evidence

*Every factual claim in this section carries an inline citation.*

- **Source-linked child blood-lead (grade A, in-country):** Among children 1–5 in three Greater Jakarta neighborhoods with informal ULAB recycling, **47% had BLL ≥5 µg/dL and 9% ≥10 µg/dL** [Suprapto et al., *Int J Environ Res Public Health* 2019;16(7):1276 — https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6480953/]. This is the single piece of direct, locally measured, source-attributable evidence in the Indonesian source profile [M3 Country 1].

- **Scale of the informal sector:** Pure Earth estimates **>200 illegal ULAB smelting locations** in Indonesia versus only ~5 licensed smelters [Pure Earth Indonesia ULAB project — https://www.pureearth.org/project/mitigating-lead-exposure-in-indonesia-used-lead-acid-batteries/]. Pure Earth separately reports **34 illegal battery-recycling facilities in the greater Jakarta area alone**, and states that **all 10 of Indonesia's worst toxic sites are battery recyclers** [National Geographic, "The Toxic Toll of Indonesia's Battery Recyclers" — https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/indonesia-s-toxic-toll].

- **Longevity of the problem:** The Committee for the Phasing Out of Leaded Fuel (KPBB) flagged the danger nearly two decades ago, identifying **71 smelters** illegally gathering and smelting used batteries, with no firm enforcement response since [as reported by The Jakarta Post, "Illegal used battery smelters imperil Greater Jakarta," 2018 — https://www.thejakartapost.com/news/2018/12/04/illegal-used-battery-smelters-imperil-greater-jakarta.html, via web search 2026-06-10]. This persistence is itself evidence that the binding constraint is enforcement, not knowledge.

- **Environmental pathway (operational description):** In informal operations, workers break open batteries, drain the acid (often onto open ground), and smelt the lead plates into ingots sold to nearby factories [National Geographic — https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/indonesia-s-toxic-toll]. Pure Earth's Toxic Sites Identification Program (TSIP) geolocates such sites as soil/dust contamination hotspots, though TSIP locates candidate sources rather than proving population-level dominance [M1-ref7; M1 limitation #6].

- **National burden context (modeled, flagged as such):** IHME GBD 2021 estimates **≈7.89 million children >5 µg/dL** nationally [IHME 2021 via Pure Earth — https://www.pureearth.org/mitigating-lead-exposure-in-low-and-middle-income-countries/]. Indonesia has **no nationally representative child blood-lead survey**; only localized studies exist (e.g., geometric mean 5.5 µg/dL in a tin-mining area, 2023) [M1-B6, M1-ref key]. The 7.89M figure is therefore modeled and cannot be apportioned between ULAB point-sources and ubiquitous consumer products without field work — see §7.

**Honest statement of what the evidence does and does not show.** It shows ULAB recycling produces severe, locally measured child lead poisoning around smelter clusters. It does **not** show that ULAB is the dominant contributor to the national 7.89M count; that apportionment is exactly what M3 flags as the thing only field work can resolve [M3 Country 1, "What field testing must resolve"].

## 3. Legal / regulatory landscape

**Responsible national agency:** Ministry of Environment and Forestry — *Kementerian Lingkungan Hidup dan Kehutanan* (KLHK) — specifically its directorate-general responsible for hazardous-waste (B3) management. KLHK is the permitting and enforcement authority for B3 waste, including ULAB [SUPRA International — https://supra-international.com/insights/indonesia-s-b3-waste-management-current-status-and-challenges-in-hazardous-waste-regulation-and-implementation; the same directorate's then-director-general for hazardous waste is quoted on the smelter problem in National Geographic — https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/indonesia-s-toxic-toll].

**Governing statutes / regulations (named, with number and year):**

| Instrument | Year | What it does | Relevance to ULAB |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Government Regulation (PP) No. 22 of 2021** on Environmental Protection and Management (pollution control + B3 waste) | 2021 | Replaced PP No. 101 of 2014; sets the framework for B3 waste management, approvals, and sanctions | Spent lead-acid batteries fall under regulated B3 waste; unlicensed processing is unlawful [SUPRA International — https://supra-international.com/insights/indonesia-s-b3-waste-management-current-status-and-challenges-in-hazardous-waste-regulation-and-implementation; Enviliance ASIA — https://enviliance.com/regions/southeast-asia/id/report_5056] |
| **MoEF Regulation No. 6 of 2021** (technical procedures for B3 waste) | 2021 | Classification, storage, transport, treatment, disposal, technical approvals (PLB3 / SLO-PLB3) | Defines who may lawfully smelt/treat ULAB and under what permit [SUPRA International — same URL] |
| **MoEF Regulation No. 9 of 2024** on Management of Trash Containing Hazardous Substances and Hazardous Waste | promulgated 4 Jul 2024 | Requires individuals and businesses to **sort used batteries** and apply reduce/recycle/reuse | Brings household battery collection into a formal sorting regime — a hook for a formal off-ramp [Enviliance ASIA — https://enviliance.com/regions/southeast-asia/id/report_12264] |
| **Predecessor:** PP No. 101 of 2014 on B3 Waste Management | 2014 | Earlier B3 framework (now superseded) | Establishes the long-standing legal status of ULAB as regulated hazardous waste [SUPRA International — same URL] |

**Current enforcement status — explicit finding:** A prohibition **exists** and is reasonably clear; **enforcement is the gap.** KLHK has issued only ~5 ULAB smelting permits while >200 sites operate illegally [Pure Earth — https://www.pureearth.org/project/mitigating-lead-exposure-in-indonesia-used-lead-acid-batteries/]. The agency's own hazardous-waste director-general has stated publicly that authorities cannot simply close illegal smelters for fear of job losses and operator relocation [National Geographic — https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/indonesia-s-toxic-toll]. Unlike Egypt (where M3 found "no specific national limit for lead in ceramics"), **Indonesia's problem is the opposite of a missing standard** — this is a "law exists, enforcement does not" finding.

**Adjacent levers:** Indonesia is building formal lead-acid recycling capacity (lead-acid holds the largest share of the domestic battery-recycling market) and has high spent-battery collection rates [PS Market Research / industry reporting — https://www.psmarketresearch.com/market-analysis/indonesia-battery-recycling-market-report, via web search 2026-06-10]. State-linked actors (the Indonesia Battery Corporation: MIND ID, Antam, PLN, Pertamina) and private recyclers such as PT Nipress Tbk exist [CSIS — https://www.csis.org/analysis/indonesias-battery-industrial-strategy; industry reporting via web search 2026-06-10], meaning a formal off-ramp for collected ULAB is at least conceivable — unlike a setting with no formal capacity at all.

## 4. Supply-chain map

The informal ULAB chain in Indonesia runs from dispersed battery discards to nearby industrial buyers, and the price arbitrage at each hop is what keeps informal smelting alive despite its illegality.

| Stage | Actor | Behavior | Lever |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Generation | Vehicle owners, workshops, telecom/UPS operators | Discard spent lead-acid batteries | New MoEF Reg. 9/2024 sorting duty [Enviliance — https://enviliance.com/regions/southeast-asia/id/report_12264] |
| 2. Collection | Informal scrap collectors (*pengepul* / *lapak*) | Buy spent batteries for cash; aggregate | Could be redirected to licensed smelters if price gap closes |
| 3. Informal smelting | >200 unlicensed backyard/village smelters, concentrated in Jabodetabek (34 in greater Jakarta) | Break batteries, drain acid on open ground, smelt plates into lead ingots | The contamination node and the enforcement target [Pure Earth — https://www.pureearth.org/project/mitigating-lead-exposure-in-indonesia-used-lead-acid-batteries/; National Geographic — https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/indonesia-s-toxic-toll] |
| 4. Off-take | Nearby factories buying recyclate lead | Buy informal ingots cheaply (no environmental compliance cost embedded) | Demand-side accountability: buyers purchasing illegal-source lead |
| 5. Formal alternative | ~5 licensed smelters; growing formal recyclers (e.g. Nipress), state-linked IBC | Permitted, higher-compliance-cost processing | The off-ramp the intervention must strengthen [CSIS — https://www.csis.org/analysis/indonesias-battery-industrial-strategy] |

The **structural difference from Bangladesh turmeric** is decisive. In Bangladesh, the adulterant (lead chromate pigment) was a *cheap additive that mills could simply stop using at near-zero cost* once enforcement made it risky [undark — https://undark.org/2023/07/19/the-vice-of-spice-confronting-lead-tainted-turmeric/]. In Indonesian ULAB, informal smelting is the *entire livelihood* of the operator and the *cheapest input* for the off-taker; shutting it down without a formal off-ramp pushes it underground rather than eliminating it — exactly the displacement KLHK fears [National Geographic — https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/indonesia-s-toxic-toll].

## 5. Intervention sequence (modeled step-by-step on the Bangladesh turmeric playbook)

The Bangladesh playbook had five recognized moves: (B1) credible local measurement linking the source to blood lead; (B2) media dissemination of findings; (B3) face-to-face education of the responsible businesses + 50,000 posters/flyers; (B4) handheld-XRF-enabled enforcement of an existing prohibition, including raids and fines (Shyambazar raid: ~2,000 lb turmeric seized, two wholesalers fined ~800,000 taka / >US$9,000); and (B5) follow-up monitoring that documented turmeric lead falling 47%→0% and child BLL median falling ~30% [Forsyth et al., *Environ Res* 2023 — https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0013935123011325; undark — https://undark.org/2023/07/19/the-vice-of-spice-confronting-lead-tainted-turmeric/; Bangladesh GSB summary — https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/faculty-research/publications/food-safety-policy-enforcement-associated-actions-reduce-turmeric].

| # | Indonesia ULAB step | Bangladesh analog | Adaptation / deliberate departure |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Build a georeferenced ULAB contamination map paired with child-BLL sampling.** Deploy handheld XRF on soil, dust, slag, and battery casings at Jabodetabek informal smelter clusters, and draw paired child blood-lead in the same households, across smelter and non-smelter areas. | B1 — Stanford/icddr,b measured turmeric lead *and* linked it to mill-worker/consumer BLL before any enforcement | **Adaptation:** the unit of measurement is a *site + household*, not a *market sample*. Goal is to (a) confirm the Suprapto signal at scale and (b) begin apportioning point-source vs product burden [M3 Country 1 "field testing must resolve"]. |
| **2** | **Disseminate the map through national and local media** to make the problem visibly attributable and politically costly to ignore. | B2 — news releases amplified the turmeric findings | **Direct copy**, but target Indonesian-language outlets and the EV/battery-industry narrative (Indonesia's battery ambitions [CSIS — https://www.csis.org/analysis/indonesias-battery-industrial-strategy]) so the story rides an existing policy salience. |
| **3** | **Convene the off-takers and licensed smelters, not just the informal smelters.** Face-to-face engagement with factories buying recyclate lead and with the ~5 licensed operators; distribute warning notices in smelter villages. | B3 — face-to-face meetings with key businesspeople + 50,000 posters | **Deliberate departure / addition:** Bangladesh could persuade the *producers* to stop cheaply; here the *buyers* of illegal lead are the durable pressure point, because informal smelters have no cheap substitute behavior. Add demand-side engagement Bangladesh did not need. |
| **4** | **Stand up a formal off-ramp BEFORE enforcement.** Channel informal collectors toward licensed/IBC-linked smelters and pilot a buy-back or consolidation scheme (Pure Earth has floated consolidating smelter operations at one scrubber-equipped facility [National Geographic — https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/indonesia-s-toxic-toll]), leveraging MoEF Reg. 9/2024's new sorting duty [Enviliance — https://enviliance.com/regions/southeast-asia/id/report_12264]. | *(No Bangladesh analog — explicit departure)* | **Deliberate departure, stated reason:** turmeric mills needed no economic substitute; ULAB smelters do. Skipping this step reproduces KLHK's stated displacement fear [National Geographic]. This is the single most important adaptation. |
| **5** | **Enforce the existing PP 22/2021 + MoEF Reg. 6/2021 prohibition with XRF-equipped inspections, graduated sanctions, and site remediation** at the worst sites first. | B4 — XRF-enabled raids and fines under existing food-safety law (Shyambazar: 2,000 lb seized, ~800,000 taka fined) [undark — https://undark.org/2023/07/19/...] | **Adaptation:** enforce under environmental (B3) law via KLHK, not food law; pair every closure with site remediation (TSIP-style) and worker transition, sequenced after step 4 so closures stick. |
| **6** | **Re-test and publish: repeat soil/dust XRF and child-BLL at the same sites at 12–18 months**, and report the change as the success metric. | B5 — follow-up showed turmeric lead 47%→0% and median child BLL −30% in 16 months [Forsyth et al. 2023 — https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0013935123011325] | **Adaptation:** soil/slag lead and child BLL at fixed cohorts replace market-sample %; expect slower decay than turmeric because environmental lead persists even after a smelter closes (soil remediation lag). |

This sequence has six ordered steps, each mapped to its Bangladesh analog or marked as a deliberate departure with a stated reason (steps 3 and 4).

## 7. Risks and unknowns (country-specific failure modes)

1. **Enforcement triggers displacement, not elimination — KLHK has said so.** The directorate-general for hazardous waste stated authorities cannot close the smelters because operators "would move to new, hidden locations" [National Geographic — https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/indonesia-s-toxic-toll]. If step 5 (enforcement) runs ahead of step 4 (formal off-ramp), the most likely outcome is dispersal of >200 sites into harder-to-find ones. This is the dominant, Indonesia-specific failure mode and the reason the sequence inverts the Bangladesh order.

2. **The point-source / national-burden apportionment may be smaller than assumed.** ULAB is grade-A *near smelters* but its share of the modeled 7.89M national count is unknown, because Indonesia has no national child-BLL survey [M1-B6; M3 Country 1]. If consumer products (cookware 51% over threshold in the regional RMS; ceramics 45%) actually drive the population mean [Bortey-Sam et al., *Sci Rep* 2024 — https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-59519-0; M3 Country 1], a ULAB-only program could collapse the worst local exposures yet barely move the national figure — and be judged a failure on the wrong metric.

3. **Livelihood politics scuttle closures.** Informal smelting is the operators' entire income and the off-takers' cheapest input [National Geographic — https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/indonesia-s-toxic-toll; §4]. Job-loss politics already stayed KLHK's hand for ~20 years since KPBB's first count of 71 smelters [The Jakarta Post 2018 — https://www.thejakartapost.com/news/2018/12/04/illegal-used-battery-smelters-imperil-greater-jakarta.html]; a closure program without worker transition risks the same stall.

4. **Decentralized enforcement and permitting capacity.** KLHK has issued only ~5 ULAB permits against >200 illegal sites [Pure Earth — https://www.pureearth.org/project/mitigating-lead-exposure-in-indonesia-used-lead-acid-batteries/], indicating the formal permitting/monitoring channel has limited throughput; even with political will, inspection capacity across the Jabodetabek industrial fringe may be the binding constraint.

5. **Environmental lead persists after closure (slow success signal).** Even where the Bangladesh model produced a 47%→0% turmeric-lead drop in ~2 years [Forsyth et al. 2023 — https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0013935123011325], soil/slag lead at former smelter sites does not vanish on closure; the step-6 re-test may show muted child-BLL improvement without active remediation, risking a premature "it didn't work" verdict. Tied to the documented open-ground acid-draining and slag accumulation at informal sites [National Geographic — https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/indonesia-s-toxic-toll].

6. **Formal off-ramp could be captured by the EV/nickel narrative.** Indonesia's battery-industrial strategy is oriented toward EV/lithium and nickel downstreaming [CSIS — https://www.csis.org/analysis/indonesias-battery-industrial-strategy]; lead-acid ULAB is the unglamorous legacy stream and risks being deprioritized by the very state-linked actors (IBC/Pertamina) best placed to provide the off-ramp.

## 8. Limitations & counter-evidence

1. **Single primary blood-lead study.** The grade-A child-BLL signal rests on one cross-sectional Greater Jakarta study [Suprapto et al. 2019 — https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6480953/], now seven years old and geographically narrow. It establishes that ULAB *can* poison children locally; it does not establish national prevalence or trend.

2. **No national child-BLL survey exists**, so the 7.89M figure anchoring the burden is modeled (IHME) and inherits M1's modeling-circularity caveat [M1 limitation #2; IHME via Pure Earth — https://www.pureearth.org/mitigating-lead-exposure-in-low-and-middle-income-countries/]. A reader should treat the national magnitude as estimated, not measured.

3. **Choosing ULAB over consumer products is contestable.** M3 ranks ULAB #1 by *likelihood of dominance* and grade-A evidence, but explicitly warns that a "severe-but-local" source can outrank a "ubiquitous-but-mild" one in the table while carrying a *smaller* share of national burden [M3 limitation #2]. A funder optimizing for population-attributable fraction might target cookware/ceramics instead; this dossier follows the spec's mandate to dossier the *top-ranked source*, and flags the disagreement honestly.

4. **Several supply-chain and enforcement facts come from journalism, not peer review.** Smelter counts (34 in greater Jakarta; all top-10 toxic sites being battery recyclers; the KLHK official's quote) come from National Geographic and The Jakarta Post [https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/indonesia-s-toxic-toll; https://www.thejakartapost.com/news/2018/12/04/illegal-used-battery-smelters-imperil-greater-jakarta.html]; the >200 vs ~5 figure is Pure Earth's own estimate [https://www.pureearth.org/project/mitigating-lead-exposure-in-indonesia-used-lead-acid-batteries/]. These are credible but not independently verified counts.

5. **Regulation citations are at the framework level.** PP 22/2021 and MoEF Reg. 6/2021 are confirmed via legal-summary secondary sources [SUPRA International; Enviliance ASIA — URLs above], not the gazetted Indonesian-language texts; the specific clause classifying ULAB and the exact sanction schedule should be verified against the primary regulation before any enforcement action is built on this dossier. The Jakarta Post quote was retrieved via web-search summary (2026-06-10), as direct fetch of several sources returned HTTP 403.

6. **Source staleness.** The KPBB 71-smelter count and the KLHK official's quote date to ~2018 [The Jakarta Post 2018]; the named director-general may no longer hold the post, and the current count of illegal sites may differ. The Bangladesh results are 2021 [Forsyth et al. 2023]. None of the on-the-ground figures here are 2025–2026 vintage.

7. **English-language / NGO-linked source bias** carried from M1–M3 [M1 limitation #7]: Indonesian-language KLHK enforcement records, court actions, and provincial environmental-agency data are under-represented; "not found" means not found in this search.
