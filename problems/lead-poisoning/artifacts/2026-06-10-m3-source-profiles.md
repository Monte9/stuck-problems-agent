# M3 — Source-Attribution Profiles for the Top 3 Countries

**Date:** 2026-06-10
**Milestone:** M3 (Source-attribution profiles for Indonesia, Egypt, Philippines)
**Primary inputs:** `artifacts/2026-06-10-m2-priority-shortlist.md` (the priority shortlist; top 3 = Indonesia, Egypt, Philippines) and `artifacts/2026-06-10-m1-data-landscape.md` (the data inventory). Bracketed `[M2]` and `[M1-Bn]`/`[M1-refN]` pointers refer to those artifacts. New source-specific evidence gathered for this milestone carries its own inline citation.
**Question being answered:** For each top-3 country, which candidate exposure sources are most likely to be a *dominant* contributor to child lead burden, how good is the evidence, and where should the first field XRF/sampling effort point?

A framing caveat carried from M2: the goal of field work in these three is mostly **deepening** (linking measured sources to measured blood lead, and finding the dominant channel) rather than greenfield discovery, because Pure Earth's LEAF program already operates in all three [M2 §3; Pure Earth, "Mitigating Lead Exposure in LMICs" — https://www.pureearth.org/mitigating-lead-exposure-in-low-and-middle-income-countries/]. Source ranks below are *likelihood of dominance*, not *certainty*.

---

## Shared evidence-grade legend

| Grade | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **A** | **Direct local measurement** — the source has been physically measured (product XRF/lab, environmental sampling, or source-linked blood lead) *within the country in question*. |
| **B** | **Regional or analogous evidence** — measured in a comparable setting (neighbouring country, same product category in a multi-country program that included this country but is not disaggregated, or this country's regulatory/trade data implying presence) but not directly demonstrated to dominate child exposure here. |
| **C** | **Inference only** — plausible on first principles or by global pattern, but no measurement specific to this source in this country was found. |

Rank = author's ordinal judgment of *likelihood of being a dominant contributor* to child lead burden (1 = most likely dominant). Rank and grade are independent: a high-confidence (A) source can still be a minor contributor, and a plausibly-dominant source can be poorly evidenced (C).

## Country 1 — Indonesia

The Indonesian signal is unusually clear on one source and unusually blank on the rest. Informal used lead-acid battery (ULAB) recycling has *direct, source-linked child blood-lead measurement* (grade A); every consumer-product channel rests on a regional multi-country program (RMS) that sampled Indonesia but is not publicly disaggregated by country (grade B), or on inference (grade C).

| Rank | Source | Grade | Key evidence (with citation) | Suggested first XRF / field targets |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Informal ULAB battery recycling** | **A** | In a cross-sectional study of children 1–5 in three Greater Jakarta (Jabodetabek) neighbourhoods with informal ULAB recycling, **47% of children had BLL ≥5 µg/dL and 9% ≥10 µg/dL** [Suprapto et al., *Int J Environ Res Public Health* 2019;16(7):1276 — https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6480953/]. Pure Earth estimates **>200 illegal ULAB smelting locations** vs only ~5 licensed [Pure Earth — https://www.pureearth.org/project/mitigating-lead-exposure-in-indonesia-used-lead-acid-batteries/]. | Soil/dust XRF inside and around informal smelter villages in Jabodetabek and Java industrial corridors; battery casings, slag piles, and recyclate; child BLL in the same households. |
| 2 | **Cookware / metal foodware** | **B** | Indonesia was one of the 25 LMICs sampled in Pure Earth's Rapid Market Screening; in the pooled multi-country result **metal foodware was the single most-contaminated category (51% of samples over threshold)** [Bortey-Sam et al., *Sci Rep* 2024;14:9251 — https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-59519-0; M1-B6, M1-ref4]. Country-level Indonesia figures are not publicly disaggregated, so this is regional-grade for Indonesia specifically. | Aluminium/tinned pots, kettles, and food-contact metalware in Jakarta, Surabaya, and Bandung wet markets and hardware stalls; leach-test the highest XRF readings. |
| 3 | **Paint (decorative/enamel)** | **B** | Indonesia has a LEEP paint program and was in the RMS, where **41% of paints exceeded the 90 ppm limit across the 25 countries** [Pure Earth RMS, *Sci Rep* 2024 — https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-59519-0; LEEP — https://leadelimination.org/2025-in-review/; M1-B6, M1-ref5]. Indonesia-specific paint exceedance rates were not found in this search, so regional-grade. | Solvent-based enamel/gloss decorative paints (yellow/red/green pigments) in Jakarta/Surabaya hardware markets; school and playground paints. |
| 4 | **Ceramics / glazed foodware** | **B** | RMS pooled result: **ceramic foodware 45% over 100 ppm** [Bortey-Sam et al., *Sci Rep* 2024 — https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-59519-0]. No Indonesia-disaggregated ceramic figure found; graded regional. | Glazed earthenware bowls/plates and traditional cooking pots in local pottery markets (e.g. Java/Bali artisanal producers); leach-test glazes. |
| 5 | **Cosmetics** | **B** | RMS pooled cosmetics **12% over threshold** [Bortey-Sam et al., *Sci Rep* 2024 — https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-59519-0]; broader LMIC cosmetics contamination documented [Brown et al., "True Colors," *Environ Health Perspect* 2022;130(4) — https://ehp.niehs.nih.gov/doi/full/10.1289/EHP9220]. No Indonesia-specific cosmetic XRF found. | Coloured powder cosmetics, traditional/herbal cosmetics, and skin-lightening creams in urban markets and online resellers. |
| 6 | **Spices / food adulteration (turmeric)** | **C** | Lead-chromate turmeric adulteration is measured across **South Asia** (Bangladesh, India, Pakistan, Nepal), not Indonesia [Forsyth et al., *Sci Total Environ* 2024 — https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0048969724051532]. Indonesia consumes turmeric but no Indonesia-specific adulteration measurement was found — pure inference here. | Whole vs powdered turmeric and curry blends in traditional markets; XRF + lead:chromium ratio to flag lead chromate. |

**Indonesia "point the XRF here first":** Lead the campaign at **(1) informal ULAB smelter villages in the Jabodetabek/Greater Jakarta industrial fringe** — soil, dust, slag, and co-located child BLL — because this is the only grade-A source and the one with a remediation/enforcement pathway; and **(2) metal cookware and glazed ceramic foodware in Jakarta and Surabaya wet markets**, the two highest-prevalence consumer categories in the regional RMS that have never been disaggregated for Indonesia. Market type to sample: dense urban wet markets and hardware/houseware stalls plus the residential smelter clusters.

**What field testing must resolve that desk research cannot:** Whether ULAB — clearly dominant *near smelters* — is the dominant source of the **national** burden, or only a severe local one, with the remaining millions of >5 µg/dL children [IHME 2021, ~7.89M — https://www.pureearth.org/mitigating-lead-exposure-in-low-and-middle-income-countries/] driven by ubiquitous consumer products (cookware, ceramics). Desk data cannot apportion the 7.9M modeled count between point-source and product-source channels; only paired product-XRF-plus-household-BLL sampling across smelter and non-smelter areas can.

**Indonesia limitations note:** Only ULAB carries grade-A, source-linked blood-lead evidence; all four consumer-product ranks lean on RMS pooled numbers that are *not* Indonesia-disaggregated in the public paper, so their relative rank within Indonesia is genuinely uncertain. There is no national child blood-lead survey to anchor any apportionment [M1-B6].

## Country 2 — Egypt

Egypt's best primary evidence is a single Cairo cross-sectional study that points hardest at **lead-glazed pottery / occupational pottery work** and at **legacy leaded-gasoline soil/dust** (both grade A, locally measured). Paint and kohl cosmetics are documented sources in the region with weak Egyptian product-testing, and spices/water are inference. Public product-testing in Egypt is thin [M1-B10], so several ranks here are deliberately graded down.

| Rank | Source | Grade | Key evidence (with citation) | Suggested first XRF / field targets |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Lead-glazed pottery / ceramics (incl. occupational pottery)** | **A** | In a community-based study of Cairo children, **pottery-workshop children had the highest mean BLL, 43.3 µg/dL**, with workshop-area environmental lead above guidelines [Mansour & Mossa, "Environmental and Occupational Lead Exposure Among Children in Cairo," *J Health Pollut* / PMC — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4782899/]. Egypt has **no specific national limit for lead in ceramics** [Egypt lead-regulation profile, Global Lead Forum 2021 — https://globalleadforum.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/country_profile_egy.pdf]. | Glaze XRF on traditional fired earthenware (water jars/"qulla", cooking and serving ware) in pottery districts (e.g. Fustat/Old Cairo, Fayoum); soil/dust and child BLL in pottery-worker households. |
| 2 | **Leaded-gasoline legacy soil & dust** | **A** | The same Cairo study identifies **contaminated dust and soil from accumulated leaded-gasoline residue** plus lead paint in dilapidated housing as the most common exposure routes, with residential-area lead exposure significantly correlated with elevated BLL [Mansour & Mossa, PMC4782899 — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4782899/]. | Roadside and inner-courtyard surface-soil/house-dust XRF in older high-traffic Cairo districts; hand-wipe and floor-dust sampling in homes of young children. |
| 3 | **Kohl / traditional eye cosmetics** | **B** | Kohl (eye cosmetic, applied to infants in the region) is a recognised, frequently lead-rich exposure source, and is named as a candidate source in the Cairo study; the broader evidence base on traditional eye cosmetics as a lead route is regional/global, not an Egypt product-XRF dataset [Mansour & Mossa, PMC4782899 — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4782899/; AAP, "Traditional Eye Cosmetics and Cultural Powders as a Source of Lead Exposure," *Pediatrics* 2024;154(Suppl 2) — https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/154/Supplement%202/e2024067808O/199570/]. | XRF on loose kohl/kuhl powders sold in souks (Khan el-Khalili and neighbourhood markets) and pharmacies; survey infant-application practices. |
| 4 | **Paint (decorative/enamel)** | **B** | Egypt's **Ministerial Decree No. 381/2022** limits lead in non-coloured base paints to 100 ppm but **still permits up to 5,000 ppm in coloured paints** — a weak limit that leaves coloured enamels legal [chemycal summary of Decree 381/2022 — https://chemycal.com/news/191407ec-9c82-4531-8f9e-767be6e0eb19/; UNEP 2021 status — https://www.unep.org/resources/report/2021-update-global-status-legal-limits-lead-paint]. Lead paint in older housing is named as a common Cairo source [PMC4782899]. No public Egyptian paint-XRF exceedance dataset found, so regional-grade. | Coloured solvent-based enamels (yellow/red/green) in Cairo hardware markets; old-housing wall paint chips and window/door enamels in low-income districts. |
| 5 | **Spices / food adulteration** | **C** | Egyptian spice studies report toxic-element and adulterant concerns [Egyptian spices heavy-metals/dye study, PMC 2025 — https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12727808/], and Egypt has **no national lead limit for spices** [Global Lead Forum 2021 profile — https://globalleadforum.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/country_profile_egy.pdf]. But lead-chromate turmeric adulteration is measured in South Asia, not Egypt — Egyptian dominance is inferred [Forsyth et al., *Sci Total Environ* 2024 — https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0048969724051532]. | Bulk-bin turmeric, chili, and paprika in spice souks; XRF + lead:chromium ratio. |
| 6 | **Water / canned food / other** | **C** | The Cairo study lists canned food/juice and food-handling practices as possible sources; no quantified Egypt drinking-water or canned-food lead dataset was found in this search [PMC4782899 — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4782899/]. | Solder seams on canned goods; first-draw tap water in old buildings with legacy plumbing. |

**Egypt "point the XRF here first":** Lead with **(1) lead-glazed traditional pottery/ceramics** (water jars and cooking/serving ware) in Cairo and Fayoum pottery districts, and **(2) urban surface soil and household dust** in older high-traffic Cairo neighbourhoods — the two grade-A channels from the only solid local study — sampled in **artisanal pottery markets/workshops and low-income inner-city residential districts.** Add kohl powders from souk vendors as the strongest grade-B add-on.

**What field testing must resolve that desk research cannot:** Egypt's burden ranking in M2 rests largely on a *modeled adult cardiovascular* (lead-IHD) metric, not a child blood-lead count [M2 §3; M1-B10]. There is **no national child BLL survey**, so desk research cannot even confirm the magnitude of childhood exposure, let alone its dominant source. Field work must first establish a representative child-BLL baseline and then test whether pottery/soil (severe but possibly localised) or a ubiquitous channel (paint, kohl) drives the population mean.

**Egypt limitations note:** Two of the six ranks (spices, water/canned) are pure inference, and the primary local evidence is essentially one cross-sectional Cairo study that is now a decade old, so the present-day mix may have shifted (e.g. post-2022 paint decree, leaded-gasoline phase-out maturing). Public Egyptian product-XRF data is the thinnest of the three countries.

## Country 3 — Philippines

The Philippines is the inverse of Indonesia/Egypt: its **consumer products are already directly XRF-screened in-country** (a Pure Earth market survey, grade A), so several product ranks are grade A, while the missing piece is national, source-linked blood-lead measurement.

| Rank | Source | Grade | Key evidence (with citation) | Suggested first XRF / field targets |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Cosmetics** | **A** | Pure Earth/EcoWaste 2021–22 market survey (17 markets, 144 vendors, 856 samples) found **33% of cosmetics above lead thresholds — the highest exceedance rate of any category tested** [Pure Earth Philippines Lead Briefer 2024 — https://www.pureearth.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/PE-PH-Lead-Briefer_2024.pdf; M2 §3]. | Skin-lightening creams, coloured/loose powder cosmetics, and traditional cosmetics in Metro Manila public markets and online resellers; XRF + confirmatory lab on the worst hits. |
| 2 | **Cookware / metal foodware** | **A** | Same survey: **24% of metal cookware over threshold** [PE-PH Lead Briefer 2024 — https://www.pureearth.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/PE-PH-Lead-Briefer_2024.pdf]. | Aluminium/recycled-metal pots, kettles, and food-contact metalware in Divisoria and provincial wet markets; leach-test. |
| 3 | **Informal ULAB battery recycling** | **A** | Pure Earth has worked Philippine ULAB sites since 2008; **>150 toxic sites assessed, >25% lead-contaminated**, with the Marilao–Meycauayan–Obando river system and former Meycauayan smelter sites flagged for severe ULAB lead pollution affecting nearby residents [Pure Earth Philippines — https://www.pureearth.org/philippines/; Pure Earth/UNIDO TSIP Philippines report — http://www.pureearth.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/04/Philippines-TSIP-Report-UNIDO.pdf]. | Soil/dust and slag XRF at Meycauayan/Marilao (Bulacan) ULAB clusters; child BLL in adjacent barangays. |
| 4 | **Paint (decorative/enamel)** | **A** | Market survey: **16% of household paints over threshold** despite a national ban; FDA has flagged 97+ leaded spray paints and EcoWaste keeps finding banned additives in spray paints [PE-PH Lead Briefer 2024 — https://www.pureearth.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/PE-PH-Lead-Briefer_2024.pdf; EcoWaste/FDA spray-paint enforcement — https://journal.com.ph/ecowaste-coalition-uncovers-12-more-spray-paints-with-banned-lead-additives/]. | Imported and spray enamels (especially yellow/red) in hardware stalls; school/playground repaint surfaces. |
| 5 | **Ceramics / glazed foodware** | **A** | Same survey: **13% of ceramic foodware over threshold** [PE-PH Lead Briefer 2024 — https://www.pureearth.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/PE-PH-Lead-Briefer_2024.pdf]. | Glazed plates/bowls and decorative foodware in markets; leach-test glazes. |
| 6 | **Spices / food adulteration** | **C** | No Philippine spice lead-adulteration measurement found; lead-chromate turmeric adulteration is a South Asian phenomenon and the Philippines is not a major turmeric-consuming or producing market [Forsyth et al., *Sci Total Environ* 2024 — https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0048969724051532]. Inference only. | Imported turmeric/curry blends in markets if screening capacity allows; low priority. |

**Philippines "point the XRF here first":** Because products are already screened, lead field work at **(1) cosmetics (skin-lightening creams and loose powders) and (2) informal ULAB recycling clusters in Bulacan (Meycauayan/Marilao)** — the highest-exceedance product category and the most severe point source respectively — sampled in **Metro Manila public markets / online-reseller pickups for cosmetics, and the Bulacan smelter barangays for ULAB (soil + child BLL).** Cookware is a close third.

**What field testing must resolve that desk research cannot:** The Philippines knows *what is contaminated on the shelf* but not *what is in the children's blood* — there is no recent nationally representative child BLL survey [M1-B9]. Desk research cannot link the 33%-contaminated cosmetics or 24%-contaminated cookware to actual child exposure or rank them against ULAB point-source exposure. Paired product-use surveys plus household child-BLL sampling are required to convert shelf-prevalence into an attribution.

**Philippines limitations note:** Spices is pure inference and ranked last for that reason. The grade-A product figures are 2021–22 [PE-PH Lead Briefer 2024] and predate any enforcement response, so current exceedance rates may differ. ULAB site data (TSIP) geolocates hotspots but does not by itself establish population-level dominance [M1 limitation #6].

## Limitations & counter-evidence

1. **Grade B is doing heavy lifting for Indonesia.** Four of Indonesia's six ranks (cookware, paint, ceramics, cosmetics) rest on the RMS *pooled* 25-country result, which is **not publicly disaggregated by country** [Bortey-Sam et al., *Sci Rep* 2024 — https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-59519-0]. It is entirely possible Indonesia's cookware exceedance is far above or below the 51% pooled figure; the within-Indonesia ordering of these four is therefore weakly supported. This is the single biggest soft spot in the artifact.

2. **Rank ≠ dominance, and severe-but-local can outrank ubiquitous-but-mild in the table while being a smaller share of national burden.** ULAB (Indonesia #1, Philippines #3) produces extreme local BLLs but may affect a smaller population than a mild, ubiquitous product like cookware. The ranks express *likelihood of being dominant* but cannot, without field apportionment, prove which channel carries the larger share of the national child-lead burden. A reviewer optimizing for population-attributable fraction might reorder.

3. **Egypt's primary evidence is one decade-old Cairo study.** The pottery (43.3 µg/dL) and soil/dust findings come from a single cross-sectional study [PMC4782899 — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4782899/]; Cairo pottery workshops may not represent national exposure, and the leaded-gasoline soil signal will have been decaying since the phase-out. Egypt's two grade-A ranks are geographically and temporally narrow.

4. **Spices graded C everywhere — possibly too low.** The turmeric-adulteration literature is strong but **South-Asia-specific** [Forsyth et al., *Sci Total Environ* 2024 — https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0048969724051532]. I declined to import a grade-A South Asian finding into Indonesia/Egypt/Philippines (per the legend, no grade-A claim may rest solely on another country's data). If global spice supply chains carry adulterated turmeric into these markets, spices could be under-ranked — but absent local measurement, C is the honest grade.

5. **Program-presence selection bias persists.** The Philippines looks best-evidenced partly *because* Pure Earth/EcoWaste already sampled it [PE-PH Lead Briefer 2024]; Egypt looks worst-evidenced partly because little has been published, not necessarily because exposure is lower. Evidence grade tracks *where programs have looked*, which is correlated with, but not identical to, where the lead actually is.

6. **Water/plumbing largely unassessed in all three.** None of the three had usable local drinking-water lead data in this search; water is folded into Egypt's grade-C "other" row and omitted as a standalone rank for Indonesia/Philippines. This is a genuine blind spot — legacy plumbing and lead solder could be a quiet contributor that no desk source here captures.

7. **English-language / NGO-linked source bias carried from M1 and M2** [M1 limitation #7]: "no Indonesia/Egypt-specific data found" means none found in this search, not none exists; Indonesian- and Arabic-language environmental-agency datasets are under-represented.
