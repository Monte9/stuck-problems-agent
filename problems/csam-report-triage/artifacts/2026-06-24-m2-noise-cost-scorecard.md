# M2 — Per-Sender Noise-Cost Scorecard ("the noise tax")

**Problem:** CSAM Report Triage — turning 21M CyberTipline reports into a ranked rescue docket
**Milestone:** M2 — Per-sender noise-cost scorecard
**Date:** 2026-06-24

---

## 1. What this scorecard measures

The CyberTipline's failure mode is not too few reports — it is too many *low-actionability* reports burying the actionable ones. NCMEC and law enforcement must touch every report that arrives, but only some carry the "who/what/where/when" (the M1 rubric's D3–D5, plus the file D1 and human-review flag D2) that makes a child rescuable. The **noise tax** is the triage labor a sender imposes that yields no investigable lead: high volume + low actionability = high burden.

This scorecard pairs each named sender's **report volume** (a reported fact, from NCMEC's per-ESP data or congressional disclosures) with an **actionability/quality indicator** (where one is public) and characterizes the resulting noise tax. It applies the M1 rubric's logic — completeness on the offender-identity (D3), geolocation (D4), and human-review (D2) dimensions — wherever sender-level field data exists. For most senders, NCMEC publishes *volume* but not *field-level quality*; the 2025 Grassley/Senate Judiciary disclosure is the first time per-sender actionability percentages were made public for several platforms, and those are used as the actionability column where available.

### Ranking criterion (one sentence, re-applicable)

> **Senders are ranked by estimated non-actionable report count = reporting volume × (1 − actionability rate); where no actionability rate is published, the sender is placed by volume and flagged as quality-unknown, because an unmeasured large stream is itself a triage liability.**

The non-actionable count is the directly burden-relevant quantity: it is the number of reports a sender pushes into the queue that, by the receiver's own assessment, cannot be worked. (Volume alone over-penalizes Meta, which is both the largest reporter and substantially actionable; actionability alone over-penalizes a tiny sender with 0% useful reports.)

## 3. Methods note — reported facts vs. author's estimates

**Reported facts (taken directly from a cited public source, not computed by the author):**
- All **2024 volume figures** are read directly from NCMEC's *2024 CyberTipline Reports by Electronic Service Providers* PDF (http://ncmec.org/content/dam/missingkids/pdfs/cybertiplinedata2024/2024-reports-by-esp.pdf). Exact values extracted: Facebook 8,590,357; Instagram 3,320,008; WhatsApp 1,851,086; TikTok 1,359,806; Google 1,175,084; Snapchat 1,174,698; X Corp 686,176; Discord 241,354; MediaLab/Kik 114,155; Microsoft–Online Operations 101,009 (+Xbox 1,324, +other 324); Pinterest 65,810; Dropbox 48,342; Amazon Photos 42,051; OpenAI 32,079; Amazon AI Services 30,759; Roblox 24,522; Anthropic 971; Amazon (core) 390.
- All **2025 volume figures** (Meta ~11M, TikTok 3.6M+, Amazon AI Services 1.1M, Snapchat 752k+, X.AI 135k+, Grindr 111k+, Roblox 65k+, Discord ~490k) and all **actionability percentages** (Amazon 0%, Snapchat >80% inactionable, X.AI >90% inactionable, Grindr 4% with location / down from 35%) are quoted from the Senate Judiciary Committee (Grassley) release and the NCMEC 2025 first-look blog.
- The **system totals** (21.3M reports, 61.8M files, 17M/81% from the eight senders, 20.5M in 2024, 36.2M in 2023, ~50% "informational" in 2023) are reported facts from the cited NCMEC/Thorn pages.

**Author's estimates (computed; assumptions stated):**
- **"Est. non-actionable reports"** = `volume × (1 − actionability_rate)`.
  - *Amazon AI Services* (~1,100,000): not really an estimate — NCMEC states **0%** were actionable, so volume × 1.00. Assumption: "no actionable information" = 100% non-actionable.
  - *Snapchat* (~602,000): 752,000 × 0.80. **Assumption:** the ">80% inactionable" rate, which NCMEC reports *only for the subset that received law-enforcement feedback*, is applied to the full 2025 volume. This likely **overstates** the count if feedback-eliciting reports were a non-representative (worse) sample; flagged accordingly.
  - *X.AI* (~122,000): 135,000 × 0.90, using the **pre-remediation** ">90% inactionable" rate. **Assumption:** counts the original burden before X Corp. escalation added location data; the post-remediation count is lower and not quantified here.
  - *Grindr* (~106,000): 111,000 × 0.96. **Assumption:** "only 4% contained location information" is used as a proxy for actionability (geolocation, M1 D4, being the limiting field NCMEC names); a report can lack location yet carry a usable identifier, so this proxies *non-actionability* and may **overstate** it.
- All **"Unknown"** cells (TikTok, Meta rate, Discord rate, Google, Microsoft, OpenAI) are deliberately left uncomputed: no per-sender actionability rate is public, and inventing one would violate the sourcing rule. These senders are ranked by **volume** with a quality flag, per the stated criterion.
- **M1-rubric linkage:** where field-level deficiencies are named (Amazon/Discord = missing location + suspect info → D3/D4 absent; Grindr = 4% location → D4 absent; Snapchat = "not useful" → low composite), the scorecard maps the deficiency to the corresponding M1 dimension. No full M1 0–100 score is computed per sender, because NCMEC publishes deficiency *descriptions*, not the per-field presence data the rubric needs.

## 4. Limitations & counter-evidence

**Data-availability gaps (senders/figures where quality data does not exist):**
1. **Most large senders publish no field-level quality data at all.** Google (1.18M reports, 2024), Microsoft (~103k), Pinterest, Dropbox, OpenAI, Anthropic, and Amazon Photos appear in NCMEC's volume table but have **no public actionability rate**. Their "Unknown" cells are a data gap, not a finding of low noise — a high-volume sender could be a major silent noise source we cannot rank. The 2025 Grassley actionability percentages exist for only 8 senders because a Senate committee compelled them; there is no routine public per-sender actionability disclosure.
2. **Actionability rates are non-comparable across senders.** Snapchat's ">80% inactionable" is measured on *reports that drew law-enforcement feedback*; Amazon's "0%" is NCMEC's own assessment of the whole stream; Grindr's "4%" measures *location-field presence*, not end-to-end actionability; X.AI's ">90%" is *pre-remediation*. These are four different denominators and definitions. Computing a single "non-actionable count" across them (my ranking metric) silently assumes they are commensurable, which they are not — the ranking should be read as ordinal and approximate, not as a precise leaderboard.
3. **Year mismatch between volume and actionability.** Several rows pair a **2025** actionability statement with the only citable **2024** per-ESP volume (e.g. Snapchat 2024 = 1.17M but 2025 = 752k; Discord 2024 = 241k but 2025 = ~490k). Volume moved materially year-over-year (Meta bundling/E2EE cut totals from 36.2M to 20.5M between 2023 and 2024), so non-actionable counts computed from mixed-year inputs carry real error. I used the same-year figure where both exist and flagged the mismatch otherwise.

**Counter-evidence / scope caveats:**
- **"Non-actionable" ≠ "should not have been sent."** A report that cannot be investigated today may still matter: it can corroborate a known offender, feed hash databases, or satisfy the mandatory-reporting statute (M1 §1: 2258A(a) compels reporting; quality fields are discretionary under (b)). Amazon's 1.1M, though 0% actionable for rescue, did surface CSAM inside AI training data — a genuine harm signal even if it names no victim. The noise tax is a *triage-capacity* cost, not a verdict that the reports were illegitimate.
- **Volume can fall for good reasons.** The 2023→2024 decline was driven partly by Meta *bundling* (explicitly a noise-reduction feature) and by end-to-end encryption reducing what platforms can detect — so lower volume is simultaneously less noise *and* less visibility into real abuse. A pure "less volume = less noise tax" reading would be wrong.
- **The estimates over-penalize the worst-disclosed.** Because actionability data exists only where it is bad enough to draw congressional scrutiny, the senders with computed non-actionable counts (#1–4) are a *biased sample* of the worst cases; quiet senders with no disclosure may have comparable noise that this scorecard cannot surface (gap #1). The ranking is therefore conservative about absence and harsh about disclosure — the opposite of what fairness to senders would require, and the right bias for a triage-burden analysis but the wrong one for naming-and-shaming.
- **Source recency/auditability:** 2025 figures are from NCMEC's 2026 first-look and a 2025/26 Senate Judiciary release (current as of this artifact, 2026-06). 2024 per-ESP figures are from NCMEC's official PDF, extracted locally by decompressing the document's text streams (the file's text is not selectable in standard viewers); a skeptical auditor can re-extract the same values from the cited URL. The 2024-04 SIO blueprint and the ~50% "informational" 2023 figure are older than 12 months and are cited as *context/design basis*, not as current per-sender facts.

### Run-log summary

I built an 11-row per-sender noise-tax scorecard ranked by estimated non-actionable report count (volume × (1 − actionability rate)), including the required Amazon AI Services 1.1M / 0%-actionable row at rank 1, with Snapchat (>80% inactionable), X.AI (>90%), and Grindr (4% with location) as the other quantified high-noise senders, and Meta, TikTok, Discord, Google, Microsoft, OpenAI, Roblox as volume-ranked rows. Every volume cell is a reported fact — the 2024 figures extracted directly from NCMEC's per-ESP PDF (which I had to decompress locally because its text isn't selectable), the 2025 figures and all actionability percentages from NCMEC's 2026 first-look and the Grassley/Senate-Judiciary release; the only computed numbers are the four "est. non-actionable" counts, each with its assumption stated. The surprise was that the per-sender actionability data exists *at all* — it is brand-new (2025 Grassley disclosure), exists for only 8 senders because a Senate committee forced it, and the four published rates use four incompatible denominators, which is itself the cleanest evidence that the pipeline has no standard actionability metric.
