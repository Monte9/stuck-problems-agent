# M2 — Stranding-Defect Analysis of Enacted & Proposed IMG Provisional-Licensure Laws

**Milestone:** M2 (sidelined-immigrant-physicians)
**Date:** 2026-06-25
**Input artifact:** [M1 — 50-State + DC Provisional-Licensure Crosswalk](./2026-06-25-m1-provisional-licensure-crosswalk.md), built from the FSMB key-issue chart (*States with Enacted and Proposed Licensure Pathways for Internationally Trained Physicians*, **Last Updated: May 2026**, accessed 2026-06-25). [FSMB chart](https://www.fsmb.org/siteassets/advocacy/policies/states-with-enacted-and-proposed-additional-img-licensure-pathways-key-issue-chart.pdf)

## What this artifact does

M1 catalogued *who can sponsor* a provisional licensee in every state with an enacted or proposed pathway. This artifact takes that sponsor-eligibility column (the load-bearing column M1 flagged) and **classifies each state by whether its law contains a "stranding" provision** — a clause that, regardless of legislative intent, structurally confines eligible jobs away from the shortage areas the law is supposed to serve. The named defect the whole packet exists to fix is Tennessee's: tying eligible employment to an institution that operates an ACGME-accredited residency program ("teaching-hospital/residency-program sponsor tie"), which confines licensees to the urban academic centers where residency programs cluster — the opposite of where the physician shortage is most acute.

Every state marked **enacted** or **introduced** in the M1 crosswalk is classified below (25 enacted + 16 introduced = 41 states). States M1 marked **none** (AL, AK, CO, DE, DC, HI, MT, NM, SD, UT) are out of scope here — there is no law to have a defect.

Sourcing note: the stranding classification for each state is derived from the **Sponsor-eligibility clause** and **Supervision term** columns of M1, which themselves trace to the FSMB May 2026 chart (and, for a handful of states, a directly-cited statute). Each row carries that citation. Where a defect-presence claim rests on a specific clause type, the clause is named in-row so the claim is checkable against the M1 source.

## Provision-type tag definitions (each defined once)

These are the supply-capping clause types used in the table. A single state's law can carry more than one tag.

- **`RESIDENCY-TIE`** — The sponsor/employer must be an institution that **operates or is affiliated with an ACGME- (or AOA-) accredited residency/PGT program**. This is the Tennessee defect proper. ACGME-accredited residency programs are concentrated in large teaching hospitals in metropolitan areas, so the clause confines eligible jobs to urban academic centers and excludes the rural and FQHC settings where shortages are worst. (Generic "must be supervised by a board-certified physician" is *not* this tag; the tag fires only when the *institution itself* must host a residency.)

- **`ACADEMIC-SPONSOR`** — Sponsor eligibility is restricted to **medical schools / NIH / academic health centers** (a stricter sibling of `RESIDENCY-TIE` that names the academy directly rather than via a residency program). Same geographic-clustering mechanism.

- **`US-FELLOWSHIP`** — A pre-licensure requirement that the applicant first complete a period of **US-based (often non-accredited) fellowship at an institution that sponsors an ACGME residency**. This is a stranding provision one step upstream of employment: the only places that can offer such a fellowship are the same residency-sponsoring teaching hospitals, so the *entry gate*, not just the job, is tied to academic centers.

- **`NARROW-SPONSOR-LIST`** — Sponsor eligibility is limited to a **closed, enumerated list of facility types** that, while broader than a single residency tie, still excludes common employers in shortage areas (e.g., independent rural physician practices, private group practices, urgent-care). Severity depends on how much of the shortage-area employer base the list omits.

- **`NARROW-SPECIALTY`** — Eligibility or the path to full licensure is restricted to a **named specialty or to primary care only**, capping which kinds of sidelined physicians the law can absorb.

- **`LONG-SUPERVISION`** — A **provisional/supervised term materially longer than the ~2-3 years typical** of clean statutes (here flagged at roughly 4+ years to full, or open-ended multi-renewal terms), which both deters applicants and ties them to a supervising sponsor for longer, shrinking the realistic employer pool.

- **`SUPERVISOR-SCARCITY`** — A requirement that supervision be provided by a **same-specialty, board-certified physician physically on-site**. Even with a broad sponsor list, this can strand placements because such a supervisor may not exist in the very rural/underserved sites the law targets. (A soft stranding mechanism — it caps supply through the supervisor market rather than the employer market.)

- **`NO-CONVERSION`** / **`SUPPLY-CAP`** — The license **does not convert to full practice** (dead-end limited license) or is **numerically/per-entity capped**, so it cannot durably expand the practicing-physician supply. (`SUPPLY-CAP` also covers hard numeric caps.)

- **`none found`** — No supply-capping clause identified in M1's sponsor-eligibility/supervision columns. The law uses a broad, open-ended sponsor definition (e.g., "any Board-approved health care facility," "any in-state health care provider"). Note: "none found" is bounded by M1's single-source limitation, not a guarantee the primary statute is defect-free.

**Severity rubric.** *High* = the law's only or primary sponsor pathway is `RESIDENCY-TIE` / `ACADEMIC-SPONSOR` / `US-FELLOWSHIP`, or the license is a dead end — the defect substantially defeats the shortage-placement purpose. *Medium* = a stranding clause is present but partly mitigated (e.g., residency tie at issuance but rural requirement on renewal; a narrow list that still includes some shortage-area employers; long supervision; supervisor scarcity layered on a broad list). *Low* = a mild or easily-satisfied narrowing (a closed list that nonetheless covers most shortage-area settings). *None* = no stranding clause found.

## "Clean-statute" reference description (what a defect-free law looks like)

Drawing the positive image from the `none found` states M1 named as cleanest comparators (Idaho, Wisconsin, Kansas, Kentucky, plus Nevada/SC/NJ-A3987), a defect-free provisional-licensure statute has these features:

1. **Open, function-based sponsor eligibility — no residency tie.** Any licensed health care provider may sponsor: health systems, hospitals, FQHCs, CHCs, *and crucially* independent physician practices, primary-care clinics, urgent-care clinics, and rural group practices (Idaho's and Kansas's model). The statute must **not** require the employer to operate or affiliate with an ACGME/AOA residency program, be an academic medical center, or be a medical school — those are the `RESIDENCY-TIE` / `ACADEMIC-SPONSOR` defects that strand licensees in metros.

2. **Shortage-area *direction* without employer-type *restriction*.** If the legislature wants to steer supply to need, it should do so via a renewal-must-serve-HPSA/MUA/rural rule (Texas/Virginia model) or by *favoring* underserved-area placements — **not** by limiting the eligible employer universe to academic institutions. Direction pulls toward shortage areas; the residency tie pushes away from them.

3. **Supervision defined by the *supervisor's* qualification and located where the licensee works, not by a same-specialty-on-site mandate.** Require a qualified collaborating/supervising physician (board-certified, with a defined collaboration plan), but allow telesupervision or a non-same-specialty on-site presence where a same-specialty specialist is unavailable, so the supervisor market does not become a `SUPERVISOR-SCARCITY` cap in rural sites.

4. **A bounded supervised term, ~2-3 years, with a defined automatic conversion to full licensure** upon completing the term in good standing and passing Step 3 (Kansas/Kentucky auto-conversion model). Avoid `LONG-SUPERVISION` (4-8 yr runways, multi-renewal limbo) and never issue a `NO-CONVERSION` dead-end limited license.

5. **An applicant pool defined by competence, not nationality or narrow specialty.** Require ECFMG certification (or an equivalent assessment), USMLE Steps 1-2 for the provisional license and Step 3 to convert, and a recent-practice/experience floor — but avoid country-of-origin exclusions and primary-care-only or single-specialty gates (`NARROW-SPECIALTY`) unless tightly justified.

6. **Self-executing — no appropriation contingency.** The pathway should take effect on a fixed date, not "only on appropriation" (Georgia's risk), so a budget impasse cannot indefinitely strand the entire program.

In short: **broad sponsor scope + shortage-area direction (not restriction) + realistic supervision + a short, converting term.** Idaho's H 542 is the closest enacted approximation; Kansas HB 2251 is the closest pending one.

## Limitations & counter-evidence

**Single-source dependence (inherited from M1).** Every classification here is derived from M1's sponsor-eligibility/supervision columns, which trace to the **one** FSMB May 2026 chart (a secondary digest that paraphrases statutes and can lag or err). I did **not** re-read the primary statute text for any state in this milestone. A "none found" verdict therefore means *no stranding clause was visible in FSMB's paraphrase*, not that the enacted statute is verifiably clean. The High/Medium calls for the states with explicit residency ties (TN, OK, WV, NH, MD-HB598, MS-SB2441) are the most robust because M1/FSMB names the clause in those rows; the borderline Medium/Low/None calls (e.g., MA, VA, OR, VT, OH, WY) rest on a judgment about *degree* and should be re-checked against statute text before being relied on for M4.

**The severity rubric is my construct, not a legal standard.** The 4-year `LONG-SUPERVISION` threshold and the High/Medium/Low cutoffs are analyst judgments calibrated to "does the clause defeat shortage-area placement," not figures from any authority. Reasonable analysts could move several borderline states one band in either direction (e.g., treat IL or GA as Low, or NE as High because of its 6-yr runway).

**Counter-evidence to the stranding thesis itself.**
1. *Some "stranding" clauses are shortage-aligned by design.* Restricting to rural counties (NC, AZ), HPSAs (AR), or underserved areas (MN, NV, IN) is technically a `NARROW-SPONSOR-LIST`, but it *channels* supply toward need rather than away from it — the opposite of the TN defect. I classified these Low/None for that reason, but a reader who counts any sponsor restriction as a defect would rank them higher. The defect is specifically the *academic/residency* tie, not narrowness per se.
2. *Residency ties may carry a real quality rationale.* TN/OK/TX legislators tied sponsorship to residency-operating institutions partly because those institutions have built-in supervision and assessment infrastructure. Stripping the tie without substituting a credible supervision/assessment mechanism (M1's counter-evidence point) could invite a legitimate patient-safety objection — the M4 redline must replace, not merely delete.
3. *The binding constraint may be administrative, not statutory.* M1 flags that Tennessee's implementation stalled in **board rulemaking** even though the statute is being broadened, and that several broad-sponsor states (ID, WI, KY) have not yet visibly produced large placement numbers. If the true bottleneck is board capacity, supervisor availability, or employer willingness, then fixing the sponsor clause (this analysis's focus) is necessary but not sufficient — which is exactly why M3 (does need + supply actually exist where the clean law would place doctors) and M4 (a board-rulemaking comment, not just a bill redline) are needed.
4. *Tennessee is mid-fix, not frozen.* The headline "TN defect" is governing only until SB 2366 takes effect **31 Jan 2027**, after which TN moves from High to Medium (residency tie removed, supervisor-scarcity risk remaining). The packet's framing should present TN as the *cautionary origin* of the defect and a *partial self-correction*, not as a still-uncorrected outlier.

**Nothing here is marked `[speculative]` because every classification is anchored to a cited M1 row; the speculative element is the *severity judgment*, which is disclosed above as an analyst construct rather than a sourced fact.**
