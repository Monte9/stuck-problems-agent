# Problem: the sidelined immigrant physician (brain waste in a doctor shortage)

> Intake brief from the scout routine, 2026-06-25. Category: demography (migration), with an aging-demand driver.
> Bottleneck type: misaligned incentives and institutional inertia — licensing boards and the residency cartel gate a workforce the country is simultaneously short of.

**(a) The problem.** The US is projected to be short [up to 86,000 physicians by 2036](https://www.aamc.org/news/press-releases/new-aamc-report-shows-continuing-projected-physician-shortage), driven largely by aging: the 65+ population grows ~34% over that window while [one in five doctors is already 65 or older](https://www.aamc.org/news/press-releases/new-aamc-report-shows-continuing-projected-physician-shortage). At the same time, as many as [65,000 fully-trained foreign physicians live in the US and cannot practice](https://www.massmed.org/Publications/The-US-Needs-Foreign-Trained-Physicians--Why-Are-We-Making-It-So-Tough-for-Them-/) — they completed medical school and residency abroad, treated patients for years, and now drive rideshares or work as medical assistants. This is the dramatic inversion: a country rationing care while tens of thousands of doctors sit idle by rule. Across all occupations, immigrant "brain waste" costs an estimated [$39 billion in foregone wages and $10 billion in lost taxes annually](https://www.migrationpolicy.org/research/untapped-talent-costs-brain-waste-among-highly-skilled-immigrants-united-states); the physician slice converts directly into unstaffed clinics in rural and underserved counties.

**(b) The half-known solution.** Drop the redundant requirement that an already-trained, board-certified foreign physician repeat a US residency, and substitute a time-limited *provisional* license under supervision. The model is proven and cheap: Tennessee passed the [first-in-nation law in 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10688565/), requiring ECFMG certification, passed USMLE steps, a completed foreign residency, and two years of supervised practice before full licensure. Since then [at least nine states have enacted similar pathways and ~30 have introduced bills](https://www.beckershospitalreview.com/legal-regulatory-issues/30-states-have-bills-to-hire-more-foreign-trained-physicians-5-notes/). The fix costs the state essentially nothing — it removes a barrier rather than building a program — and the talent already lives here, no immigration pipeline required.

**(c) Why it's stuck.** Passing the law is not the same as opening the door. Tennessee's statute requires the sponsoring employer to *also operate a residency program*, which confines eligible jobs to large urban teaching hospitals — yet [only ~2% of Medicare-funded residency training sits in rural or underserved areas](https://www.niskanencenter.org/unlocking-potential-how-states-can-remove-barriers-for-internationally-trained-physicians/), exactly where the shortage bites. Implementation then stalled in board rulemaking; reporting indicates the [Tennessee Medical Board was slow to enforce the new law](https://www.niskanencenter.org/implementing-new-licening-pathways-that-work-for-international-doctors-and-american-patients/) well after its effective date. Each of the ~40 remaining states is writing its own incompatible rules from scratch, and federal relief — the [Healthcare Workforce Resilience Act](https://www.aha.org/news/headline/2025-09-11-congress-reintroduces-aha-supported-bipartisan-workforce-bill-supporting-foreign-nurses-physicians), which would recapture 15,000 unused physician visas — has been reintroduced repeatedly without passing. The barrier is not capability or safety data; it is institutional inertia and a residency bottleneck that functions as a supply cap. Semmelweis-shaped: the evidence (1-in-5 US doctors is already foreign-trained and outcomes are comparable) sits in plain view while the gate stays shut.

**(d) The AI-agent wedge.** Pure desk synthesis. An agent could build the artifact none of these state coalitions has: a 50-state crosswalk of provisional-licensure rules — sponsor-eligibility clauses, supervision terms, exam and residency requirements — flagging the specific provisions (the teaching-hospital tie, narrow specialty lists) that strand the law in each state. It could match county-level [physician-shortage / HPSA data](https://www.aamc.org/news/press-releases/new-aamc-report-shows-continuing-projected-physician-shortage) against estimates of sidelined IMGs by metro to quantify, state by state, how many doctors a clean statute would unlock and where. And it could draft the model-bill redline and the board-rulemaking comment that fixes the Tennessee defect — the document a legislator or medical board could act on within a session.

**(e) Closest to cracking it.** The [Niskanen Center](https://www.niskanencenter.org/unlocking-potential-how-states-can-remove-barriers-for-internationally-trained-physicians/) and [Cicero Institute](https://ciceroinstitute.org/research/overview-of-a-pathway-to-practice-for-foreign-trained-physicians/) on model legislation; [Upwardly Global](https://www.upwardlyglobal.org/) and the MIRA Coalition on the affected workforce; the [AMA's IMG advocacy](https://www.ama-assn.org/education/international-medical-education/advocacy-action-clearing-imgs-route-practice) and FSMB tracking the state chart; the AHA backing the federal visa-recapture bill. The law is moving; the synthesis that would let the next 40 states copy it *without* repeating Tennessee's mistake does not exist. That is the missing document.
