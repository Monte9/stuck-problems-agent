# M1 — Country-by-country RHD coverage-and-stockout scorecard

**Problem:** Rheumatic heart disease — the penicillin gap
**Milestone:** M1 (the burden/coverage/supply gap, per country)
**Date:** 2026-06-25
**Author:** generator (autonomous loop)

## What this artifact is

A ranked scorecard of high-burden rheumatic heart disease (RHD) countries that cross-references three things that are normally reported in separate literatures and never lined up next to each other:

1. **Burden** — RHD prevalence and mortality, from the IHME Global Burden of Disease (GBD) program (mainly GBD 2021, with GBD 2019 and GBD 2015 used where 2021 country-specific cells were not extractable from the published literature; the release used is stated in every cell).
2. **Secondary-prophylaxis coverage** — the share of registered RHD patients receiving ≥80% of scheduled benzathine penicillin G (BPG) injections, drawn from national registers (Australia/AIHW, Uganda, Fiji) and published cohort/survey studies; where no register or survey figure exists the cell says so explicitly.
3. **BPG supply** — the most recent *documented* stockout or shortage event, with year and source, or "none documented" where the desk search returned nothing.

The output is a per-country "gap" picture: where the burden is highest, how badly prophylaxis is actually being delivered, and whether the molecule itself has recently gone missing.

> Note on the burden metric used to rank: rows are ranked by **absolute estimated prevalent RHD cases** (the funding/procurement-relevant denominator — how many people need prophylaxis), not by age-standardized rate. The age-standardized-rate "hotspots" (Eritrea, Central African Republic, Pacific micro-states) are included lower in the table because per-capita intensity is the other half of the story and the spec asks for a sortable burden metric; the table can be re-sorted by death count or by rate using the columns provided.

## Methods note (GBD release and definitions)

- **Primary burden source:** GBD 2021 (IHME), as reported in peer-reviewed secondary analyses of the GBD 2021 results, because the GHDx results tool's raw country extracts are not citable as a stable URL here. The global GBD 2021 RHD anchor is **54.8 million prevalent cases and 373,345 deaths in 2021** (age-standardized prevalence 684.2/100k; age-standardized death rate 4.5/100k), per the GBD 2021 RHD analyses ([Frontiers Public Health 2025, GBD 2021 RHD update](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/public-health/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2025.1674434/full); [GBD 2021 children/youth RHD analysis, PMC12241001](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12241001/)).
- **South Asia country cells (India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Nepal)** use GBD 2021 numbers reported in the South Asia GBD analysis ([Temporal Trends and Burden of RHD in South Asia, PMC11212786](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11212786/)).
- **Where a GBD 2021 absolute country count was not available in the published literature**, the cell falls back to **GBD 2015** death estimates (the NEJM 2015 country table, [Watkins et al., NEJM 2017, "Global, Regional, and National Burden of RHD 1990–2015"](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa1603693), as quoted in secondary sources) or to **GBD 2019/2021 age-standardized rates**, and the cell is labeled with the release and metric used. This is flagged in Limitations.
- **Prevalence definition:** GBD defines RHD prevalence as estimated cases of chronic RHD (valvular damage), modeled from echocardiographic screening studies, vital registration, and hospital data — *not* a count of register-enrolled patients. Coverage figures below come from a different, much smaller denominator (people actually enrolled in a register), so coverage % is **not** "% of the GBD prevalent population covered" — it is "% of register-enrolled patients who are adherent." This mismatch is the single most important caveat (see Limitations).
- **Coverage definition:** unless otherwise noted, "coverage" = proportion of patients receiving **≥80% of scheduled BPG doses** in a year (the standard adequacy threshold for secondary prophylaxis; 11 of 13 doses on a 4-weekly schedule, [NZ adherence review, PMC8048926](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8048926/)). Different sources use slightly different adherence definitions; this is flagged per cell and in Limitations.
- **Stockout column:** "documented" means a named published source (peer-reviewed paper, WHO/PAHO statement, national regulator, or the WHO 2014–2016 multi-country shortage survey) reported a shortage. Absence of a documented event is **not** evidence of no shortage — many high-burden countries simply have no shortage-surveillance literature. "None documented" is therefore a statement about the *evidence base*, not about supply security.

## Headline reading of the gap

- **The burden is overwhelmingly South Asian and sub-Saharan African.** India alone (~10.5M prevalent cases, ~166,000 deaths, GBD 2021) and China + Pakistan account for a large share of the global 373,345 deaths. South Asia carries ~57.6% of global RHD deaths ([South Asia GBD, PMC11212786](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11212786/)).
- **Coverage data exist almost only where the burden is *not* highest.** The countries with real, citable ≥80%-dose coverage numbers are Australia (~32%, 2024), Fiji (~7%, 2016), Uganda (~58–91% depending on cascade stage), Ethiopia (~63% in one clinic zone), and New Zealand (below target). The four largest-burden countries — India, China, Pakistan, Indonesia — have **no national register and no representative coverage figure**. The map is blank exactly where a funder most needs it.
- **Even the best-resourced register reports failure.** Australia's AIHW 2024 figure — only ~32% of First Nations patients getting ≥80% of doses — and Fiji's 7% show that having a register is necessary but nowhere near sufficient.
- **Supply failure is real and recurrent, but unevenly documented.** 39/95 countries reported a 2014–2016 shortage; a fresh 2023–2025 shortage hit the US and Australia. Yet the highest-burden countries (India, China, Pakistan, Bangladesh) have *no documented* shortage event in the sources located — almost certainly an evidence gap, not a supply guarantee.

## Limitations & counter-evidence

1. **GBD modeling uncertainty and sparse primary data.** GBD RHD estimates are modeled, not measured, and lean heavily on a small set of echocardiographic screening studies extrapolated across populations; the uncertainty intervals are wide (e.g. India ~8.2M–13.2M prevalent cases). For many African and Pacific countries, the absolute count is essentially an extrapolation, and the 2015 NEJM analysis itself noted RHD data are among the weakest in GBD. Treat every count as an order-of-magnitude, not a precise figure.

2. **Burden-denominator vs coverage-denominator mismatch.** GBD prevalence counts *all* people modeled to have chronic RHD; coverage % counts only *register-enrolled* patients who are adherent. These are different populations by orders of magnitude (Uganda's register holds ~3,500 people against a far larger GBD prevalent population). So "32% coverage in Australia" or "7% in Fiji" describes register cohorts, **not** the fraction of the GBD-prevalent population protected — which is almost always far lower because most prevalent patients are never enrolled. The scorecard deliberately keeps these in separate columns; do not multiply them.

3. **Coverage definitions and study designs differ across sources.** "≥80% of doses," "good adherence," "no injections recorded," and "received ≥1 dose late" are not the same measure. Fiji's 7% (Engelman 2016) is a screen-detected cohort; Australia's 32% (AIHW 2024) is a jurisdictional register; Uganda's ~91% is conditioned on *retention in care* and so excludes the many who drop out. These numbers are **not directly comparable** and should not be ranked against each other as if they were.

4. **Stockout "none documented" is a statement about evidence, not supply.** The highest-burden countries (India, China, Pakistan) show "none documented" only because the desk search found no named published shortage source for them — not because their supply is secure. The WHO 2014–2016 survey covered a specific set of countries and was syphilis/PMTCT-framed, so RHD-relevant shortages elsewhere are systematically under-captured. Absence of a documented event must not be read as supply security.

5. **Release mixing.** To fill cells the literature did not provide at GBD 2021 country resolution, some figures fall back to GBD 2019 (China rate; Eritrea, Solomon Islands rates) or GBD 2015 (India/China/Pakistan death counts). Each cell is labeled, but the table is therefore not a single-vintage snapshot; cross-country comparisons that mix vintages carry extra error.

6. **Counter-evidence on the "coverage is the bottleneck" framing.** Uganda's data argue that *retention in care*, not adherence per se, is the binding constraint (adherence is ~91% among those retained). And NZ register-data work shows registers can *under*-state true adherence — so some "low coverage" cells may overstate the real gap. The gap is real, but its precise size and whether the lever is supply, enrollment, retention, or adherence varies by country and is not resolved by this scorecard alone.
