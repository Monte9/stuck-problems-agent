# M1 — FAP / PLS Accessibility & 501(r) Compliance Scoring Rubric + Fixed Hospital Sample

**Problem:** hospital-charity-care-gap
**Milestone:** 1 of 4 — Build the scoring rubric and fix the hospital sample
**Date:** 2026-06-23
**Scope of this artifact:** Define a transparent, machine-checkable rubric and fix the sample. **No hospital is scored here** — scoring is M2 (FAP/PLS text) and M3 (Schedule H dollars).

---

## Part A — The Rubric

### Design principles

- **Machine-checkable.** Every dimension is read from a specific public text (the FAP PDF, the Plain Language Summary, the website FAP landing page, or Form 990 Schedule H) and scored by a near-binary or banded numeric rule. No subjective adjectives ("clear," "friendly") are used as scoring criteria.
- **Grounded in law first, advocacy second.** Dimensions D1–D6 map to enumerated 501(r) regulatory requirements; D7–D9 capture accessibility/generosity concerns that the regulations leave to hospital discretion but that Dollar For, the Lown Institute, and the CFPB have documented as the actual chokepoints where eligible patients get billed.
- **Lower score = worse for the patient.** A hospital that does the legal/accessible thing earns points; an opaque or stingy one earns zero. (This is the inverse of a "non-compliance score"; M4 will invert/tier as needed.)

### Scoring conventions

- **FPL** = Federal Poverty Level / Guidelines. **AGB** = Amounts Generally Billed (501(r)(5)). **PLS** = Plain Language Summary. **ECA** = Extraordinary Collection Action (501(r)(6)). **FAP** = Financial Assistance Policy.
- "Unstated" always scores **0** for that dimension — silence is read as the worst case, because a patient (and a regulator) cannot rely on an unwritten promise. M2 must record the location it checked before assigning a 0.
- Each dimension is scored on the **single most authoritative public document available** for that facility (FAP PDF preferred over website summary; current-year over prior-year).

## Part C — Sampling Rationale

**How hospitals were chosen.** The sample mixes three axes deliberately:

1. **Geography / state-policy variation (13 states + DC-adjacent MD).** States span NC, MD, OH, MN, MA, NY, IL, GA, TX, UT, CA, MI, SD, LA, VA, MO, AR, OR, IN, WA. This spread lets later milestones compare hospitals operating under different state charity-care mandates against the federal 501(r) floor.
2. **Deliberate North Carolina over-weight (7 of 27).** NC is the natural test bed because its 2024 Medicaid-linked Healthcare Access and Stabilization / medical-debt program now requires participating hospitals to offer **free care to ≤200% FPL, 50–75% discounts to 300% FPL, and presumptive eligibility** — every eligible NC hospital signed on. NC hospitals therefore let M2 check whether published FAPs reflect a recent, specific state mandate ([NCDHHS](https://www.ncdhhs.gov/medicaldebt); [NC Governor 2024-08-12](https://governor.nc.gov/news/press-releases/2024/08/12/north-carolina-hospitals-sign-relieve-medical-debt)).
3. **System size and type.** The sample spans (a) large academic medical centers (Duke, Hopkins, Mayo, Cleveland Clinic, MGH, NewYork-Presbyterian, Northwestern, Emory); (b) very large multi-state nonprofit systems repeatedly named in Lown's fair-share-deficit analysis (CommonSpirit, Providence, Ascension, Bon Secours Mercy — [Lown 2024](https://lownhospitalsindex.org/hospital-fair-share-spending-2024/)); and (c) regional/community-anchored systems (WakeMed, Cone, ECU Health, Sanford, Intermountain, Corewell, Ochsner).

**What the sample IS representative of:** large- and mid-sized nonprofit hospital systems with established web presences and current Form 990 filings — i.e. the institutions a regulator (IRS TE/GE, a state AG) or advocate (Dollar For, Lown) is most likely to act on, and the ones whose public text actually exists to be scored. The NC subset is representative of hospitals operating under a fresh, specific state charity-care mandate.

**What the sample is NOT:**
- **Not a random or statistically representative draw** of the ~2,900 US nonprofit hospitals. It is a convenience/purposive sample skewed toward large, name-recognizable systems with discoverable FAPs, so aggregate scores cannot be generalized to "all nonprofit hospitals" or used to estimate a national non-compliance rate.
- **Not representative of small rural / critical-access hospitals,** which are under-sampled and disproportionately likely to have thin or absent web FAPs — meaning the true accessibility problem in that segment is probably worse than this sample will show (selection bias toward better-resourced institutions).
- **Not a measure of practice, only of text.** The rubric reads what hospitals *publish*; a hospital with a model FAP on paper may still bill eligible patients (the core 71%-billed gap). M2/M3 scores are necessary-but-not-sufficient evidence; they flag where the public commitment is weak, not where screening fails behind the desk.
- **Not capturing for-profit or government hospitals,** which are outside 501(r) and intentionally excluded.

## Limitations & counter-evidence

- **Text ≠ behavior (the central limitation).** The headline failure this chain targets — ~71% of eligible patients billed anyway, ~$14B/yr — happens at the point of screening and billing, which no FAP document directly reveals. A hospital can score a perfect 26 and still systematically bill eligible patients. The rubric measures *published accessibility and stated compliance*, a proxy. M2/M3 evidence is most actionable as audit-targeting signal, not proof of violation. (CFPB explicitly documents hospitals whose policies look compliant but whose collections sweep up eligible patients — [CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/about-us/blog/medical-debt-and-non-profit-hospital-billing-practices/).)
- **Threshold choices are normative, not legal.** 501(r) does **not** mandate any specific income threshold (D4/D5) or presumptive eligibility (D8); those are hospital discretion. The point bands draw their "good" benchmarks from advocacy/state standards (NC's 200%/300% FPL, Dollar For). A hospital that scores low on D4/D5/D8 may be **fully 501(r)-compliant** — the rubric deliberately scores *generosity and accessibility*, not just legality, and M4 must keep "illegal" (D1, D2, D6, D7 failures) distinct from "stingy-but-legal" (low D4/D5/D8) when assigning regulator-actionability tiers. This is the most likely line of attack from a hospital general counsel.
- **Banded scoring loses information and can mis-rank near boundaries.** A 199%-FPL hospital scores the same on D4 as a 138%-FPL one despite a real difference; M2 should record the raw number alongside the band so M4 can break ties.
- **D9's "subtract per barrier" can double-count** with D8 (an application-only hospital may also impose an asset test). The dimensions are scored independently by design, but M4's combination method must avoid penalizing the same underlying barrier twice.
- **Source recency / link rot.** FAP PDFs are revised yearly (e.g. the Cleveland Clinic FAP surfaced carries a 1/1/26 revision date) and URLs change; the 11 "confirm in M2" rows and any stale link are a known retrieval risk. M2's explicit "not found" handling is the mitigation.
- **Counter-evidence on the premise:** The American Hospital Association disputes the Lown/charity-care-deficit framing, arguing community benefit is broader than charity care and that Schedule H Line 7a understates total uncompensated care ([Keckley on the Lown–AHA dispute](https://paulkeckley.com/the-keckley-report/2024/4/1/the-lown-institute-aha-showdown-does-it-make-a-difference/)). The rubric sidesteps this by scoring FAP/PLS *accessibility* (D1–D9) on its own axis and keeping the contested dollar ratio (F1) separate, so a reader who rejects the Lown methodology can still use the M2 accessibility scores.
