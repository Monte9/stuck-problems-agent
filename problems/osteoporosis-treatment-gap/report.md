# Closing the Medicare post-fracture treatment gap: a costed CMS payment case

## Abstract

A fragility fracture in an older adult is a heart-attack-grade event, yet most Medicare patients leave the hospital with the bone fixed and the disease untreated. We asked where a single payment change could close that gap, and built the evidence package the advocacy coalition lacks: a 19-row league table of treatment rates, the benefit-harm arithmetic that inverts the safety scare, a reproducible Medicare cost model, and a docket-ready CMS comment. Three findings stand out. Organized fracture liaison services reach 68 to 80 percent treatment versus 21.1 percent for Traditional Medicare. Bisphosphonates prevent about 75 hip fractures for every atypical femoral fracture they cause. Closing the gap to a realistic 42 percent prevents roughly 3,600 refractures and 900 associated deaths a year. We recommend CMS finalize a dedicated, specialty-agnostic post-fracture care-coordination G-code in the CY 2027 Physician Fee Schedule, priced to cover the documented 105 to 182 dollars per patient of coordination cost. [1][2][3]

## Background

Osteoporotic fractures cause more Medicare hospitalizations than heart attacks, strokes, or breast cancer, and 20 to 30 percent of hip-fracture patients are dead within a year. The United States logs about 2 million fragility fractures annually, on a path to 95 billion dollars a year in costs by 2040. [4] A first fracture roughly doubles the risk of the next, and treatment is cheap and effective: generic alendronate runs about 50 dollars a year and roughly halves hip and vertebral fracture risk. The delivery model is also settled. The fracture liaison service (FLS), a nurse coordinator who catches every fracture patient and starts treatment, was invented in Glasgow in 1999 and certified in over 1,000 sites across 59 countries. A US Markov model found an FLS saves 66,879 dollars per 10,000 post-hip-fracture patients with an incremental cost-effectiveness ratio of 22,993 dollars per QALY even when FLS cost is doubled. [5] The problem is not the science. It is that no specialty owns the patient after the cast comes off, and no one is paid to close the gap. CMS took first steps in its CY 2025 rule but created no dedicated FLS payment, which is why 35 national organizations petitioned for post-fracture codes. [1]

## Method

The work ran in four stages, each a separate artifact verified by an independent evaluator before the next began. Stage one built a ranked league table of post-fracture treatment rates across 12 countries and 6 US systems from published cohorts, registries, and IOF scorecards. [6] Stage two assembled the benefit-harm dossier, reproducing the Black et al. NEJM 2020 fractures-prevented-versus-atypical-fractures arithmetic and the NNT and NNH figures. [7] Stage three rebuilt the Royal Osteoporosis Society cost model for US Medicare, with every input sourced, every formula written out, and one fully worked example. [3] Stage four converted all three into a docket-ready CMS comment and a US health-system business case template. [2] Each artifact passed a fresh-eyes evaluator who re-derived the arithmetic, spot-checked citations against source, and recorded a PASS verdict. The M3 model failed its first attempt (a hip-fracture cost was missing from the inputs table and a high-scenario figure was internally inconsistent), was corrected, and passed on the second. Artifacts and verdicts are linked throughout and listed below.

## Findings

### 1. Organized systems treat three times as many patients as Traditional Medicare, so the gap is a delivery failure, not a drug failure

The naive read blames patients or the drugs. The data blame the system. Inside an organized FLS, treatment rates reach 68 percent at Kaiser Permanente Southern California's Healthy Bones program and 72 to 80 percent at Geisinger's HiROC clinic, against 14 to 32 percent in matched usual-care patients in the same systems. [6] Traditional Medicare sits at 21.1 percent. The same patients, the same drugs, a different delivery model, and the rate triples.

| System | Treatment rate | Definition / window | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Geisinger HiROC (FLS) | 72-80% | Anti-osteoporosis med via care pathway | [6] |
| Kaiser Healthy Bones (FLS) | 68% | Pharmacotherapy after fracture, program-reported | [6] |
| England and Wales FLS-DB (national audit) | 35.4% | Med started within 16 weeks | [6] |
| US Medicare Advantage | 28.7% | New pharmacologic Rx after fracture, 2017-19 | [6] |
| US Traditional Medicare | 21.1% | New pharmacologic Rx after fracture, 2017-19 | [6] |
| US hip fracture (commercial claims) | 9.8% (2004) to 3.3% (2015) | Med within 180 days | [6] |

Rates are not directly comparable across rows: time windows range from 3 months to 12 months, denominators differ between program-reached and whole-population, and drug lists differ. The contrast between organized and unselected care holds across every caveat. The 68 and 72 to 80 percent flagship figures are the weakest-sourced in the table (Kaiser's is a 2012 conference figure, now 14 years old), so the defensible target a national rollout achieves is the UK audit's 35.4 percent, with the flagship rates as a stretch goal.

### 2. The safety scare that halved prescribing inverts a benefit-harm ratio of roughly 75 to 1

After 2010 media coverage of atypical femoral fractures, US oral bisphosphonate use fell more than 50 percent from 2008 to 2012, and post-hip-fracture initiation dropped from 9.8 percent (2004) to 3.3 percent (2015). [6][7] The arithmetic the scare ignored, from Black et al. NEJM 2020 per 10,000 women treated:

| Population | Duration | Hip fx prevented | AFFs caused | Hip-only ratio |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| White women | 3 years | 149 | 2 | ~75 : 1 |
| White women | 5 years | 286 | 8 | ~36 : 1 |
| Asian women | 3 years | 91 | 8 | ~11 : 1 |
| Asian women | 5 years | 174 | 38 | ~4.6 : 1 |

The number needed to treat to prevent one hip fracture is about 100 in secondary prevention. The number needed to harm for one atypical femoral fracture runs from about 5,750 at average exposure to about 760 at 8-plus years. [7] The duration gradient is real, which is why guidelines recommend drug holidays, not non-treatment. The post-fracture Medicare population is higher-risk than the prevalent users in Black's cohort, so the ratio runs further toward treatment for exactly the patients in question.

### 3. Closing the gap prevents about 3,600 refractures and 900 deaths a year, but whether it pays for itself depends on the payment design

Using the 21.1 percent baseline, a 42 percent target, a 35 percent on-treatment risk reduction, and 58 percent first-year persistence, the model prevents about 3,600 Medicare refractures and 900 associated deaths a year, for a gross medical offset of about 73 million dollars (blended) to 89 million (hip-weighted upper bound). [3] The honest headline is that the sign of net savings is governed by how the coordination labor is charged, not by the clinical inputs.

| Scenario | Target rate | Avoidable refx/yr | Avoidable deaths/yr | Net result/yr |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Low (conservative) | 35.4% | ~1,600 | ~400 | -203M |
| Base (whole-population cost) | 42% | ~3,600 | ~900 | -136M |
| High (targeted outreach) | 68% | ~12,000 | ~3,000 | +185M |

Charged against the entire fractured population, the base case is a net cost of about 136 million a year. Charged only on the incremental patients actually moved to treatment, the same scenario flips to net plus 185 million. The published lifetime-horizon literature (Solomon 2014, the Royal Osteoporosis Society's 3.28 to 1 return) finds the intervention cost-saving once downstream costs and QALYs are counted. [3][5] The defensible claim is not first-year budget neutrality. It is that a modest, cost-effective coordination payment unlocks a large death-and-disability reduction, and the payment design is what determines the budget sign.

## Recommendations

| Item | Executor | Cost | Anchor |
|---|---|---|---|
| Finalize a specialty-agnostic post-fracture care-coordination G-code | CMS, CY 2027 PFS | RVUs covering 105-182/patient | M3 input I14 [3] |
| File the docket comment when CY 2027 rule publishes (~July 2026) | BHOF/ASBMR/Bone Health Policy Institute coalition | comment is free | M4 Part A [2] |
| Tie a quality measure to the code | CMS | none | 21.1% baseline vs 35.4% target [6] |
| Stand up FLS programs using the business case template | US health systems / payers | 105-182/patient | M4 Part B [2] |

Sequence: the coalition files the comment the day the CY 2027 proposed rule appears (expected within weeks, file code and docket the only two fields not yet fixed), keyed to the existing 35-organization ask. [1][2] CMS sets RVUs that cover the coordination cost and does not restrict billing to primary care, the explicit failure mode of the current APCM codes since most FLS programs sit in orthopedics, rheumatology, and endocrinology. [1] Health systems that cannot wait for the code adapt the business case now, building on cost-effectiveness rather than first-year savings.

## Limitations

1. The net-dollar argument is sign-sensitive to a modeling convention, not a clinical fact. The base case is a net cost under whole-population costing and net savings only under targeted outreach. [3] First-year budget neutrality is not supported and should not be claimed.
2. Avoidable-death figures are upper-bound-leaning. Preventing a refracture does not avert the full post-fracture mortality, much of which reflects underlying frailty and competing causes. Read "900 deaths" as deaths associated with prevented refractures, not certain lives saved. [3]
3. The flagship target rates (Kaiser 68 percent, Geisinger 72 to 80 percent) are the weakest-sourced numbers, with Kaiser's being a 14-year-old conference figure. The 35.4 percent UK audit rate is the defensible national target. [6]
4. All dollar inputs are 2016 USD (Milliman) or 2014 to 2021 CEA vintages. None is 2026. Inflation means the offsets are lower bounds. [3]
5. The evidence base is overwhelmingly from women. The male benefit-harm ratio is plausibly similar but not directly established, and is marked speculative in the dossier. [7]
6. The rulemaking vehicle is not yet published. The CY 2027 proposed rule was not on the Federal Register as of 2026-06-23. The file code, docket number, and comment deadline must be confirmed from the Federal Register notice on filing day. [2]
7. The requested code does not yet exist and CMS may decline or restructure it. The coalition's view that current APCM codes are insufficient is an advocacy position, not a CMS finding. [2]
