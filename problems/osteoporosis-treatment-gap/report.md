# Closing the Medicare post-fracture treatment gap: the evidence package CMS and US payers are missing

## Abstract

A fragility fracture in an older adult roughly doubles the risk of the next one, yet only 21.1% of Traditional Medicare beneficiaries who fracture are started on anti-osteoporosis therapy. We asked where the next policy dollar against this gap should go, and built the sourced evidence package the advocacy coalition lacks: a treatment-rate league table across 12 countries and 6 US systems, a benefit-harm dossier, a reproducible Medicare cost model, and a docket-ready CMS comment plus a payer business case. Three findings stand out. Organized Fracture Liaison Services reach 68–80% treatment where unselected care reaches 3–32%. The atypical-femoral-fracture scare that halved US prescribing inverted a benefit that runs about 75 hip fractures prevented per atypical fracture caused. Closing the gap to a realistic 42% prevents about 3,600 Medicare refractures and 900 associated deaths a year. The recommendation: a dedicated, specialty-agnostic post-fracture care-coordination G-code priced to cover the documented $105–$182 per-patient cost.

## Background

A fragility fracture is a heart-attack-grade event. In the Medicare population, osteoporotic fractures cause more hospitalizations than heart attacks, strokes, or breast cancer, and the US logs over 2 million fragility fractures a year on a path to more than $95 billion in annual cost by 2040 [1]. One-year mortality is about 20% after any fragility fracture and about 30% after a hip fracture [2]. The first fracture roughly doubles refracture risk, and about 14% of fractured Medicare beneficiaries fracture again within 12 months [2].

The fix is old and cheap. Generic bisphosphonates cost on the order of $50 a year and roughly halve hip and vertebral fracture risk. The delivery model is solved too: the Fracture Liaison Service (FLS), a coordinator who catches every fracture patient and starts treatment, is cost-effective to cost-saving for US payers. A US Markov model found an FLS saves $66,879 per 10,000 post-hip-fracture patients and stays under $22,993 per QALY even at doubled program cost [3]. Yet a decade after Khosla and Shane named "a crisis in the treatment of osteoporosis," Medicare still has no dedicated FLS payment, and 35 national organizations led by BHOF and ASBMR have asked CMS to create one [1].

## Method

Four stages, each a single artifact, each evaluated by a fresh-eyes reviewer against quoted done-criteria with citation spot-checks. M1 ranked post-fracture treatment rates from cohorts, registries, and IOF scorecards [4]. M2 reproduced the Black et al. benefit-harm arithmetic and the NNT/NNH figures [5]. M3 rebuilt a Royal Osteoporosis Society-style cost model for Medicare, with every formula written out and the base case and high case re-derived by the evaluator [6]. M4 converted all three into a CMS Physician Fee Schedule comment and an NHS-style business case template [7]. M3 failed its first pass on two reproducibility defects (a missing hip-cost input and a high-scenario arithmetic error) and passed on retry; the report carries M3's honest net-cost result forward. All artifacts and verdicts live in the problem directory.

## Findings

### 1. Organized systems treat 68–80% of fracture patients; unselected care treats 3–32%

The central result inverts the naive read that treatment rates are uniformly dismal. They are dismal in unselected populations and good inside organized programs. The contrast is the policy opportunity.

| System | Type | Treatment rate | Definition / window | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Geisinger HiROC (PA) | FLS program | 72–80% (vs 14–32% usual care) | Therapy via care pathway | [4] |
| Kaiser SCAL Healthy Bones | FLS program | 68% | Post-fracture pharmacotherapy | [4] |
| England & Wales FLS-DB | National FLS audit | 35.4% | Started within 16 weeks | [4] |
| US Traditional Medicare | Population | 21.1% | New therapy after fracture, 2017–19 | [4] |
| US hip fracture (Truven) | Population | 9.8% (2004) to 3.3% (2015) | Within 180 days | [4] |

Two cautions hold. The flagship 68–80% figures are the weakest-sourced in the table: Kaiser's 68% is a 2012 conference figure, now 14 years old and not independently audited [4]. The defensible target a national rollout achieves is the UK audit's 35.4%, with 68% aspirational. And the much-quoted "3.3% treated" is a narrow 180-day oral-claims commercial figure; the same country shows 21–29% under broader Medicare definitions [4].

### 2. The safety scare that halved US prescribing was quantitatively backwards

After 2010, fear of atypical femoral fractures (AFF) drove US oral bisphosphonate use down more than 50% from 2008 to 2012, and post-hip-fracture initiation fell from 9.8% (2004) to 3.3% (2015) [5]. The arithmetic runs the other way. In White women at 3 years, bisphosphonates prevent 149 hip fractures (and 541 total clinical fractures) per 10,000 treated against 2 AFFs caused: 149 divided by 2 is about 75 hip fractures prevented per AFF [5].

| Population | Duration | Hip fx prevented | AFFs caused | Hip-only ratio |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| White women | 3 yr | 149 | 2 | ~75 : 1 |
| White women | 5 yr | 286 | 8 | ~36 : 1 |
| Asian women | 5 yr | 174 | 38 | ~4.6 : 1 |

The number needed to treat to prevent one hip fracture is about 100 in secondary prevention; the number needed to harm for one AFF runs from about 5,750 at average exposure to about 760 at 8-plus years [5]. The harm denominator is one to two orders of magnitude larger than the benefit denominator. The kernel of truth is real: AFF risk rises steeply with duration. The evidence-based answer is a time-limited course with a drug holiday, not leaving a fracture patient untreated [5].

### 3. Closing the gap prevents about 3,600 refractures and 900 deaths a year, but it does not pay for itself in year one

Using the 21.1% Medicare baseline, a target of 42%, a 35% on-treatment refracture-risk reduction, and 58% adherence, the model prevents about 3,600 subsequent fractures and about 900 associated deaths a year in the FFS population [6]. The gross medical offset is about $73M blended, up to about $89M when the costlier hip share is priced separately [6].

| Cost-charging basis | Program cost / yr | Gross offset / yr | Net result / yr |
|---|---|---|---|
| Whole-population FLS staffing | ~$209M | ~$73M | **−$136M (net cost)** |
| Targeted outreach (treated only)* | ~$56M | ~$242M | **+$185M (net saving)** |

*The targeted high scenario also raises the target to 68% and adherence to 65% [6].

The honest headline: under whole-population costing the base case is a net cost of about $136M a year, not a saving. The sign of the net result is governed by how the coordination labor is charged, not by the clinical inputs [6]. The supportable claim is cost-effectiveness, not first-year budget neutrality. The peer-reviewed CEAs that use a lifetime horizon find FLS cost-saving [3], and the Royal Osteoporosis Society's England model returns £3.28 per £1 invested [6]. That accounting question, what you pay for the coordinator, is precisely the question CMS is being asked to settle.

## Recommendations

| Item | Executor | Cost | Anchor |
|---|---|---|---|
| File a CY 2027 PFS comment for a dedicated post-fracture care-coordination G-code | BHOF/ASBMR coalition | Staff time; comment is drafted [7] | 35-org coalition letter [1] |
| Price the code to cover FLS coordination | CMS | $105–$182 per patient | M3 input I14 [6] |
| Make the code specialty-agnostic | CMS | none | Coalition objection to primary-care-only codes [1] |
| Adapt the business case to launch a payer FLS | US health systems | Per-patient $105–$182 | M4 Part B [7] |

Sequencing is time-bound. The CY 2027 PFS proposed rule was not published as of 2026-06-23; on the standard CMS calendar it appears about July 2026 with a 60-day comment window [7]. The comment is ready now except for two identifiers (file code and docket number) that do not yet exist and must be filled from the Federal Register notice on filing day [7]. The coalition should file inside that window, build on CMS's CY 2025 first-step codes, and press the one objection that matters: primary-care-anchored codes miss the orthopedics, rheumatology, and endocrinology programs that actually run FLS [1, 7]. The business case template runs in parallel and needs no rulemaking.

## Limitations

1. The avoidable-deaths figures are upper-bound-leaning. Preventing a refracture does not avert the full post-fracture mortality, much of which reflects underlying frailty and competing causes. Read "~900 deaths" as deaths associated with prevented refractures, not certain lives saved [6].
2. The net-dollar result is sign-sensitive to a modeling convention, not a clinical fact. Whole-population costing gives −$136M; targeted outreach gives +$185M [6].
3. The flagship target rates (Kaiser 68%, Geisinger 72–80%) are the weakest-sourced numbers carried forward; 35.4% is the defensible national-rollout target [4].
4. All dollar inputs are 2016-USD (Milliman) or 2014/2021 CEA vintages; none is 2026, so offsets are lower bounds [6].
5. The benefit-harm evidence is overwhelmingly from women; the male-specific ratio is plausibly similar but not directly established and is flagged speculative [5, 7].
6. The rulemaking vehicle is not yet published; if CMS's calendar slips, the commenter must re-confirm the rule's identifiers and deadline [7].
