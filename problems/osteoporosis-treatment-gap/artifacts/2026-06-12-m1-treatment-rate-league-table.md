# M1 — League table of post-fracture osteoporosis treatment rates

**Problem:** the post-fracture osteoporosis treatment gap
**Milestone:** M1 | **Date:** 2026-06-12 | **Status:** draft for evaluation

## Purpose and how to read this table

This table compares the share of fragility-fracture patients who receive pharmacologic anti-osteoporosis treatment afterward, across countries and US health systems/states. The canonical metric is *treatment initiation within ~12 months of fracture*, but few sources report exactly that; the **Treatment definition & time window** column records what each source actually measured. Ranking is by headline reported rate, descending — treat the rank as indicative, not exact, because definitions differ (see Comparability caveats). Rows marked **[program]** report rates inside an organized Fracture Liaison Service (FLS)-type program; rows marked **[population]** report unselected population or payer-wide rates. The contrast between the two is itself the central finding: organized systems reach 68–80%, unselected populations sit at 3–40%.

### The US decline, documented (done-criterion row)

Row 19 ([Desai et al., JAMA Netw Open 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6324295/)) provides both required data points from a single consistent series: **9.8%** of hip-fracture patients initiated osteoporosis medication within 180 days in **2004** (pre-2010) and **3.3%** in **2015** (post-2013). Row 10 ([Solomon et al., JBMR 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4258070/)) corroborates with a 12-month metric: **40.2% (2002) → 20.5% (2011)**. The widely quoted "15% (2004) → 3% (2013)" formulation (e.g., [Pain News Network, 2016](https://www.painnewsnetwork.org/stories/2016/6/24/doctors-warn-of-crisis-in-osteoporosis-treatment)) appears to be a rounding/intermediate-year rendering of the same family of claims analyses; the peer-reviewed series above should be preferred for citation.

### European context

The [IOF SCOPE 2021 scorecard](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8172408/) estimates that across the EU27+2, **71% of women eligible for osteoporosis therapy were untreated in 2019** (14.8M women), up from a 55% gap in 2010 — a different metric (eligible-but-untreated, not post-fracture initiation), but consistent with the table's direction: the gap widened during the 2010s even as fracture burden grew.

## Comparability caveats

These rates are **not directly comparable** across rows. Specific reasons:

1. **Time windows differ by a factor of four or more.** Korea/Spain/US-Medicare (rows 4, 11, 17) use a 3-month window; Desai (row 19) uses 180 days; most others use 12 months; the UK FLS-DB uses 16 weeks; ANZHFR (row 5) measures medication *at hospital discharge* only. Longer windows mechanically produce higher rates — Japan's series shows 23.0% at injury rising to 47.3% at 12 months within the same cohort ([Inage et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40592642/)).
2. **Denominators differ: program-reached vs. population.** Geisinger (row 1) and the UK FLS-DB (row 6) count only patients an FLS actually assessed; patients never captured by the service are invisible to the numerator and denominator. Kaiser's 68% (row 2) is a program-reported figure from a system with near-total membership capture, but is still self-reported by program champions rather than independently audited. Population rows (Sweden, Denmark, Medicare) count everyone, including the never-referred.
3. **"Treatment" means different drug lists.** Japan's 47.3% counts active vitamin D3 analogues (~60% of its treated patients) — excluded from nearly every Western definition; the Manitoba row counts HRT for women 65+; Kim et al. include calcitonin and raloxifene; Desai counts only bisphosphonates, teriparatide, and denosumab. The Danish study is one of the few that explicitly captures hospital-administered zoledronic acid/denosumab via procedure codes — claims-only studies elsewhere can undercount infusion/injection therapies (e.g., Part B-administered drugs in some US datasets).
4. **Fracture-type mix differs.** Hip-only cohorts (rows 3–5, 7, 10, 14, 16, 17, 19) capture the oldest, frailest patients, in whom treatment rates run lower; all-fragility or MOF cohorts (rows 6, 9, 12, 15, 18) mix in wrist/humerus patients who are younger and likelier to be treated — or, in the FLS-DB case, the reverse selection (hip patients partly routed to a different audit).
5. **Data sources differ: claims vs. registry audit vs. facility survey vs. program self-report.** Claims (filled prescriptions) miss in-hospital starts and free samples; facility surveys (Japan) rely on clinician report; clinical audits (FLS-DB, ANZHFR) depend on voluntary hospital participation and data completeness; program self-reports (Kaiser, Geisinger) have publication and champion bias.
6. **Populations differ in sex and age.** Several rows are women-only (Manitoba, Azad's Medicare cohorts) or men-only (VHA); men are consistently treated at roughly half the rate of women where both are reported (Hawaii 11.9% vs 19.3%; Taiwan ~20% vs ~40%; Sweden 5–9% vs 8–17%), so single-sex rows are biased relative to mixed rows.
7. **Data vintages span 1997–2024.** Rates moved substantially within single jurisdictions over time (US: 40.2%→20.5% down; ANZHFR: 20%→37% up; UK FLS-DB: 30.0%→35.4% up), so cross-row comparisons conflate place with period.

## Limitations & counter-evidence

- **The headline program rates (rows 1–2) are the weakest-sourced numbers in the table.** Kaiser's 68% comes from a 2012 conference presentation reported by a trade outlet ([Healio](https://www.healio.com/news/orthopedics/20120325/osteoporosis-prevention-program-effective-in-reducing-hip-fracture-rates)), not a peer-reviewed denominator-explicit study, and is now 14 years old; no current audited Kaiser post-fracture treatment rate was located in this pass. Geisinger's 72–80% partly rests on a conference abstract. If M3 uses these as the "achievable rate," it should stress-test against the UK FLS-DB's much lower 35.4% — which is what a *national* FLS rollout actually achieves at audit, not what a flagship program achieves.
- **Counter-evidence to the "collapse" narrative: several systems are improving.** UK FLS-DB treatment-within-16-weeks rose 30.0%→35.4% (2022→2023); ANZHFR discharge medication rose 20%→37% (2016→2024); Japan's 2022 pay-for-performance fee raised post-hip-fracture prescribing by [20.8 percentage points](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41091162/); Denmark's gap narrowed slightly (85%→79%). The decline story is strongest in the US 2002–2015 claims data and should not be projected globally or assumed to continue past 2015 without newer evidence.
- **The most-cited US soundbite ("only 3% treated") is a 180-day, oral-claims, commercial-population figure.** The same country shows 21.1–28.7% in 2017–2019 Medicare data with a broader definition ([Azad et al. 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39570337/)). Honest advocacy should quote the range with definitions, not the minimum.
- **Two cells carry definitional softness, flagged inline:** the Azad rows' time window is not stated in the publicly available abstract (full text paywalled), and the Germany row is a literature-synthesized range (20–40%) rather than a single national cohort estimate — no single national German post-fracture initiation cohort with a clean 12-month figure was located in this pass.
- **Source-access failures:** the Australian Institute of Health and Welfare osteoporosis page returned HTTP 403, so Australia is represented by the ANZHFR at-discharge metric rather than a 12-month national dispensing figure; a 2025 JGIM Medicare FFS study ([Wu et al.](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11606-025-10090-y)) defines the right outcome (any OP medication within 1 year of MOF, 2017–2020 fractures) but its headline percentage sits behind a paywall and could not be extracted.
- **Vintage risk:** Canada's row is 2001/02 data — among the oldest here — and likely misstates current Canadian practice in either direction; Manitoba later ran a notification program that roughly doubled odds of post-fracture care ([Leslie et al., 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31267162/)). Korea/Spain/Taiwan rows are 2006–2015 vintage.
- **No row is presented as the current state of 2026 practice.** The most recent population data points in this table are 2019 (US Medicare), 2020 (Sweden, Japan survey), 2023 (UK audit), and 2023–24 (ANZHFR). Everything else is historical context.
- [speculative] The table likely *understates* treatment in systems where zoledronic acid is given during the index hospitalization and recorded only in hospital billing (not pharmacy claims) — plausibly relevant to the very low Desai 3.3% figure, since post-2017 US guidelines encourage inpatient zoledronic acid after hip fracture; no source quantifying this undercount was identified in this pass.

## Implications for M3 (forward pointer, not a finding)

For the Medicare cost-of-the-gap model: the defensible **baseline** is the 2017–2019 Traditional Medicare rate of **21.1%** post-fragility-fracture treatment ([Azad et al. 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39570337/)) — or the more conservative hip-only ~11–17% band (rows 14, 17) — and the defensible **target** band is 35.4% (UK national FLS audit, row 6) to 68–80% (flagship US programs, rows 1–2), with sensitivity analysis spanning that range.
