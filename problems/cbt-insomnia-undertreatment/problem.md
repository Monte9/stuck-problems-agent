# Chronic insomnia: the first-line treatment almost nobody gets

**Domain:** Mental health and loneliness

## The problem, in plain language

Chronic insomnia — trouble sleeping at least three nights a week for three months
or more — affects roughly 6–10% of adults, with clinically relevant prevalence
estimated as high as 16.2% globally and rising in older adults
([Sleep Med Reviews 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12676268/)).
It is not just tiredness. RAND estimates the United States loses over 1% of GDP —
about $207.5 billion a year — to insomnia-driven productivity loss, with affected
workers losing 45–54 workdays annually
([RAND RRA2166-1](https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RRA2166-1.html)).
The drugs most patients are handed instead of the recommended treatment actively
harm them: in older adults, z-drugs (zolpidem, zopiclone) raise fracture risk
(hazard ratio 1.67, hip fracture 1.96) and are tied to falls, stroke, and cognitive
decline; guidance says to avoid them in dementia, yet they are still prescribed
([Age and Ageing 2018](https://academic.oup.com/ageing/article/47/2/201/4564456);
[BMC Medicine 2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12916-020-01821-5)).

## The half-known solution

Cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) is the guideline-recommended
first-line treatment — more durable than hypnotics and without the harms. The
dramatic inversion: the free, drug-free therapy outperforms the pills that are
actually prescribed. Fully automated **digital CBT-I** now makes it scalable. A
2025 matched-control study found digital CBT-I (SleepioRx) cut first-year
healthcare costs by **$2,083 per patient (42%)**, driven by a 46% drop in pharmacy
spend ([PMC12619666](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12619666/)). A 2025
decentralized RCT confirmed effectiveness in US adults
([JMIR Mental Health 2025](https://mental.jmir.org/2025/1/e84323)). Stepped-care
models let nurses and GPs deliver the first step, reserving scarce specialists for
non-responders ([Sleep Medicine 2025](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1389945725002266)).

## Why it's stuck

Despite decades of strong recommendations, **fewer than 10% of insomnia patients
receive CBT** ([JMIR 2025](https://mental.jmir.org/2025/1/e84323)). The bottleneck
is grind and diffusion, not invention: too few trained providers, poorly
distributed; low clinician awareness; and a clinical Catch-22 where chronic
insomnia is defined by ≥3 months but hypnotic labels cap use at ~4 weeks, so
prescribers default to repeat scripts they are hesitant to stop
([Front Public Health 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/public-health/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2023.1233201/full)).
This is a textbook overdue diffusion: a proven, cheap, harm-reducing therapy
sitting unused while a more expensive, more harmful default persists — the
H. pylori pattern applied to sleep.

## The AI-agent wedge

A model running unsupervised for hours can produce the missing **synthesis
artifact** that a payer or formulary committee needs to act. Concretely: (1) a
quantified comparison of insomnia-prescribing patterns vs. CBT-I availability,
pulling published prevalence, prescription-volume, and provider-supply data into
one defensible cost-of-inaction figure per population; (2) a head-to-head dossier
of the now-reimbursable digital CBT-I products (Somryst, Sleepio/SleepioRx) with
their trial evidence, FDA status, and the 2025 CMS reimbursement codes, so a payer
can move; (3) a deprescribing-and-substitution brief targeting the highest-harm
group — elderly long-term z-drug users — pairing the harm evidence with the
stepped-care substitution pathway. All of this is desk-research over published
literature and regulatory documents; no new trial required.

## Who's closest today

Big Health (Sleepio/SleepioRx) and the makers of Somryst hold FDA clearances, and
**CMS in 2025 established national reimbursement codes for FDA-cleared digital
mental-health treatments** — the single biggest unlock in years
([PMC12619666](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12619666/)). The American
Academy of Sleep Medicine sets the guidelines; the VA has run some of the largest
CBT-I dissemination programs. The gap is no longer reimbursement or evidence — it
is the unglamorous synthesis that converts "covered and proven" into "ordered by
the clinician in front of the patient." That document does not yet exist at the
specificity a payer or health system needs, and that is the wedge.
