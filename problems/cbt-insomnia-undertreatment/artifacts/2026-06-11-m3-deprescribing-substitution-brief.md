# M3 — Deprescribing-and-substitution brief: adults 65+ on long-term z-drugs/benzodiazepines

**Problem:** cbt-insomnia-undertreatment · **Milestone:** 3 · **Date:** 2026-06-11
**Audience:** prescribers, pharmacists, and nurses in primary care; health-system pharmacy leads designing a deprescribing program
**Builds on:** [M1 cost-of-inaction baseline](./2026-06-11-m1-cost-of-inaction-baseline.md) (hypnotic harm inputs, Component C) and [M2 product/reimbursement dossier](./2026-06-11-m2-digital-cbti-product-reimbursement-dossier.md) (which digital CBT-I products exist and how Medicare pays for them since 2025-01-01).

**Bottom line:** For adults 65+ using benzodiazepine receptor agonists (BZRAs: benzodiazepines and z-drugs) most nights for more than ~4 weeks, deprescribing is a **strong recommendation** of the 2018 evidence-based Canadian guideline ([Pottie et al., *Can Fam Physician* 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5951648/)) and both drug classes are "avoid" medications in the [2023 AGS Beers Criteria (DOI 10.1111/jgs.18372)](https://agsjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/jgs.18372). The best-evidenced exit is **gradual taper plus CBT-I**: in randomized trials of older long-term users, taper+CBT-I roughly doubles discontinuation versus taper alone (85% vs 48% drug-free, [Morin 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14754783/); 77% vs 38%, [Baillargeon 2003](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC236226/)), and even a mailed self-help package more than triples discontinuation versus usual care (26.2% vs 7.5% at 6 months; [YAWNS NB, *JAMA Psychiatry* 2024, DOI 10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2024.2731](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapsychiatry/fullarticle/2823668)). Every step below can be delivered by a named role — pharmacist, nurse, primary-care prescriber, or a prescription digital program — without requiring the scarce behavioral-sleep-medicine workforce quantified in M1 (~0.26 trained providers per 100,000 adults).

## 1. One-page harm summary: what continued BZRA use costs a 65+ patient

| # | Adverse outcome | Effect size (95% CI) | Population / design | Source (year) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Falls** | Benzodiazepines: OR **1.57** (1.43–1.72); sedatives/hypnotics: OR **1.47** (1.35–1.62) | Meta-analysis, 79,081 participants ≥60 | [Woolcott et al., *Arch Intern Med* 2009, PMID 19933955](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19933955/) |
| 2 | **Fractures (z-drugs)** | OR **1.63** (1.42–1.87); zolpidem risk rises sharply with age (>300% increase in users over 85) | Meta-analysis, 14 studies, older adults | [Treves et al., *Age and Ageing* 2018, DOI 10.1093/ageing/afx167](https://academic.oup.com/ageing/article/47/2/201/4564456) (M1 Row 10) |
| 3 | **Hip fracture and stroke in dementia** | Higher-dose z-drugs: hip fracture HR **1.96**, any fracture HR 1.67, stroke HR 1.88 vs untreated sleep-disturbed comparators | Population-based cohort, people living with dementia, England | [Richardson et al., *BMC Medicine* 2020, DOI 10.1186/s12916-020-01821-5](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12916-020-01821-5) (M1 Row 11) |
| 4 | **Motor-vehicle crash (older drivers)** | Long-half-life benzodiazepine: RR **1.45** (1.04–2.03) in first 7 days; RR **1.26** (1.09–1.45) with continued use up to 1 year. Short-half-life: no increase (RR 1.04, 0.81–1.34). *Counter-evidence:* a pooled subgroup of drivers >65 across mixed benzodiazepines was not statistically significant (OR 1.13, 0.97–1.31) | Cohort/within-person, 224,734 Quebec drivers aged 67–84; meta-analysis subgroup | [Hemmelgarn et al., *JAMA* 1997, PMID 9207334](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9207334/); counter-evidence [Dassanayake et al., *Drug Saf* 2011, PMID 21247221](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21247221/) |
| 5 | **Cognitive impairment (current long-term use)** | Impaired vs controls in **all** domains tested; mean weighted effect size **−0.74** (range −1.30 to −0.42) | Meta-analysis, 13 neuropsychological studies of long-term users | [Barker et al., *CNS Drugs* 2004, PMID 14731058](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14731058/); deficits persist after withdrawal per updated meta-analysis [Crowe & Stranks, *Arch Clin Neuropsychol* 2018, DOI 10.1093/arclin/acx120](https://academic.oup.com/acn/article/33/7/901/4734935) |
| 6 | **Dementia / Alzheimer's disease** | Ever-use OR **1.51** (1.36–1.69); >180 prescribed daily doses OR **1.84** (1.62–2.08), dose-response. (Reverse causation not excluded — see Limitations) | Case-control, 1,796 AD cases / 7,184 controls, age >66, Quebec | [Billioti de Gage et al., *BMJ* 2014, DOI 10.1136/bmj.g5205](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.g5205) (M1 Row 12) |

**Benefit on the other side of the ledger is small:** the 2023 Beers Criteria rate z-drugs as producing "minimal improvement in sleep latency and duration" in older adults while carrying benzodiazepine-like harms (delirium, falls, fractures, ER visits, motor-vehicle crashes), and recommend avoiding both classes — explicitly noting shorter-acting benzodiazepines are *not* safer ([AGS 2023 Beers Criteria, *J Am Geriatr Soc* 2023, DOI 10.1111/jgs.18372](https://agsjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/jgs.18372)).

**The substitute works in this age group:** in a 3-arm RCT of 311 adults aged 55–95 (mean ~66; 31% over 70), the digital CBT-I program SHUTi OASIS (the older-adult adaptation of the program underlying Somryst) produced large ISI improvements (Cohen's d −1.04 to −1.43 across timepoints) versus small-to-medium changes with online patient education, with greater reductions in sleep-medication use and 83% program completion ([*npj Digital Medicine* 2025, DOI 10.1038/s41746-025-01847-0](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-025-01847-0)). Guidelines already designate CBT-I first-line for chronic insomnia in all adults ([ACP 2016, Qaseem et al., *Ann Intern Med*, DOI 10.7326/M15-2175](https://www.acpjournals.org/doi/10.7326/M15-2175); [AASM behavioral-treatment guideline 2021, Edinger et al., *J Clin Sleep Med*, DOI 10.5664/jcsm.8986](https://jcsm.aasm.org/doi/10.5664/jcsm.8986)).

## 2. Stepped substitution pathway: taper + CBT-I, with a named deliverer at every step

The pathway assembles the [Pottie 2018 deprescribing algorithm](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5951648/) (also published as the [deprescribing.org BZRA algorithm](https://deprescribing.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/08/Deprescribing-focus-on-BZRAs-FINAL-August2018_CC.pdf)) with the direct-to-patient trials (EMPOWER, YAWNS NB, D-PRESCRIBE), the taper+CBT-I RCTs (Morin 2004, Baillargeon 2003), and the M2 reimbursement mechanics.

| Step | Action | Who delivers it | Evidence source |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Case-finding** | Identify adults 65+ dispensed a BZRA most days/nights for >4 weeks from dispensing records or claims (any duration qualifies at 65+ under the guideline) | **Community pharmacist** (dispensing records) or **health-plan pharmacy team** (claims query) | Eligibility per [Pottie 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5951648/) (strong recommendation, ≥65); pharmacy-based identification as operationalized in [EMPOWER](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24733354/) and [D-PRESCRIBE](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6248132/) |
| **2. Direct-to-patient activation** | Hand or mail a patient education package: the EMPOWER brochure or, preferably, the Sleepwell package (adds CBT-I self-help content). Effect of the package *alone* at 6 months in 65+ long-term users — EMPOWER trial (N=303): 27% discontinuation vs 5% usual care (risk difference 23%, NNT 4). YAWNS NB (N=565, mean 11.4 yrs of BZRA use): discontinuation 26.2% (Sleepwell) vs 20.3% (EMPOWER package) vs 7.5% (usual care); ≥25% dose reduction in a further 20.4% / 14.4% / 12.8% | **Community pharmacist** (EMPOWER, D-PRESCRIBE model) or **mailed by health plan/practice** (YAWNS NB model — no clinician time required) | [Tannenbaum et al., EMPOWER, *JAMA Intern Med* 2014, PMID 24733354](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24733354/); [Murphy et al., YAWNS NB, *JAMA Psychiatry* 2024, DOI 10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2024.2731](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapsychiatry/fullarticle/2823668); package materials at [mysleepwell.ca](https://mysleepwell.ca/) and [deprescribing.org](https://deprescribing.org/news/empower-trial-empowering-older-adults-to-reduce-benzodiazepine-use/) |
| **3. Pharmacist→prescriber opinion** | Pharmacist sends the prescriber an evidence-based "pharmaceutical opinion" recommending deprescribing with a proposed taper. In D-PRESCRIBE (N=489, mean age 75), this plus the patient brochure achieved **43.2% vs 9.0%** sedative-hypnotic discontinuation at 6 months (risk difference 34%, 95% CI 25–43%) | **Community pharmacist** (sender); **primary-care prescriber (GP/NP)** (recipient/authorizer) | [Martin et al., D-PRESCRIBE, *JAMA* 2018, DOI 10.1001/jama.2018.16131](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6248132/) |
| **4. Shared decision + eligibility check** | Confirm the BZRA is for insomnia (not anxiety, seizures, or alcohol withdrawal — see Section 3); screen for untreated sleep/psychiatric/physical comorbidity; agree on a quit plan and start date | **Primary-care prescriber (GP or nurse practitioner)**, using the algorithm's engagement script | [Pottie 2018 algorithm](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5951648/) (patient engagement + exclusions); [deprescribing.org algorithm PDF](https://deprescribing.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/08/Deprescribing-focus-on-BZRAs-FINAL-August2018_CC.pdf) |
| **5. Start CBT-I before or alongside the taper** (stepped options, lowest-intensity first): (a) **prescription digital CBT-I** — SleepioRx or Somryst, ordered within an ongoing behavioral-health plan of care so Medicare pays via G0552/G0553/G0554 (M2 §2); older-adult RCT evidence d −1.04 to −1.43 (SHUTi OASIS); (b) **brief behavioral treatment for insomnia (BBTI)** — 4 weeks, 2 visits + 2 phone calls; in 79 older adults (mean age 71.7), response 67% vs 25%, remission 55% vs 13%, NNT 2.4, delivered by a master's-level mental-health nurse practitioner with *no prior sleep-medicine experience*; (c) **group/individual CBT-I, 8–10 weekly sessions** — the format used in both taper-combination RCTs | (a) **Digital program**, prescribed by **GP/NP**, billed by the **behavioral-health clinician or PCP** managing the plan of care; (b) **nurse or nurse practitioner**; (c) **psychologist or trained therapist** | (a) [M2 dossier §2](./2026-06-11-m2-digital-cbti-product-reimbursement-dossier.md), [SHUTi OASIS RCT, *npj Digit Med* 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-025-01847-0); (b) [Buysse et al., *JAMA Intern Med* 2011, PMID 21263078](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3101289/); (c) [Morin 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14754783/), [Baillargeon 2003](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC236226/) |
| **6. Written gradual taper** | ~25% dose reduction every 1–2 weeks; slow to ~12.5% steps near the end; planned drug-free nights before full stop; total duration weeks-to-months. **Do not substitute** another sedating drug (antihistamines, trazodone-as-hypnotic, quetiapine) — the guideline recommends behavioral management, not pharmacological switching. Never stop abruptly. In Morin 2004 (10-week protocol, mean 19.3 years of use), 63% were drug-free within ~7 weeks with **no significant withdrawal adverse events** | **Primary-care prescriber (GP/NP)** writes the schedule; **pharmacist** co-develops dosing steps (D-PRESCRIBE model) and can dispense partial/reduced doses | Taper parameters per [Pottie 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5951648/); taper safety per [Morin 2004, PMID 14754783](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14754783/); 25% q1–2wk schedule as used in [Baillargeon 2003](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC236226/) |
| **7. Monitoring during taper** | Contact every 1–2 weeks (visit or phone): withdrawal symptoms (anxiety, irritability, sweating, rebound insomnia — expect them to be transient), mood, sleep, benefits (alertness, cognition). If bothersome symptoms: hold the current dose 1–2 weeks, then resume; use a withdrawal symptom questionnaire if needed | **Pharmacist or nurse** (the guideline names physicians, psychologists, pharmacists, and nurses as able to deliver monitoring); escalate to the prescriber only if symptoms persist | [Pottie 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5951648/) (monitoring schedule and personnel) |
| **8. Relapse / non-response** | If taper fails or insomnia persists after CBT-I: re-attempt later at slower pace — dose reduction alone counts as partial success (+11% dose-reduction beyond discontinuation in EMPOWER; +20.4% in YAWNS NB Sleepwell arm); refer persistent complex insomnia to a **sleep specialist / behavioral sleep medicine provider**; refer suspected sedative use disorder or co-occurring substance use to **addiction medicine** | **Sleep specialist / BSM provider**; **addiction-medicine specialist** for use-disorder features | [EMPOWER](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24733354/) and [YAWNS NB](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapsychiatry/fullarticle/2823668) (dose-reduction outcomes); referral logic per [Pottie 2018 exclusions](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5951648/); abuse/dependence risk per [FDA benzodiazepine boxed-warning communication, Sept 2020](https://www.fda.gov/media/142368/download?attachment=) |

**Why combine, in one line of numbers:** taper alone got 48% (Morin) / 38% (Baillargeon) of older long-term users off BZRAs; adding CBT-I raised that to **85%** / **77%** post-treatment, with 70% vs 24% still off at 12 months in Baillargeon (blood-test confirmed). The CBT-I component in those trials was 8–10 weeks of therapist-delivered treatment; steps 5(a)–(b) substitute scalable delivery (digital program with ~20–40 min/month of billable clinician management under G0553/G0554, per M2; nurse-delivered BBTI totaling ~2.5 hours) for the therapist bottleneck M1 quantified.

## 3. Contraindications and when NOT to apply this pathway

1. **BZRA prescribed for something other than insomnia.** The deprescribing recommendation applies to BZRAs taken for primary insomnia or comorbid insomnia with managed underlying conditions. It explicitly does **not** apply when the drug treats an anxiety disorder, seizure disorder, alcohol withdrawal, or another condition requiring specialist management — those patients need psychiatric/neurological co-management, not this pathway ([Pottie 2018, exclusions](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5951648/)).
2. **Untreated comorbidity driving the insomnia.** Other untreated sleep disorders (obstructive sleep apnea, restless legs, parasomnias) and untreated depression, anxiety, or physical conditions causing or aggravating insomnia must be assessed and managed first ([Pottie 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5951648/)). Screen for OSA before CBT-I: sleep restriction can worsen unrecognized apnea-related sleepiness.
3. **Sleep-restriction safety cautions.** The sleep-restriction component of CBT-I acutely reduces total sleep time and objectively impairs vigilance in the first 1–3 weeks (psychomotor-vigilance lapses increased at 3 of 5 assessment points, effect sizes 0.69–0.78, returning to baseline by 3 months — [Kyle et al., *SLEEP* 2014;37(2):229–237, PMID 24497651](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3900612/)). Use caution or a supervised/modified protocol in: epilepsy (sleep loss lowers seizure threshold), bipolar disorder (sleep loss can trigger mania), excessive daytime sleepiness, high fall risk, and patients who must drive or operate machinery — counsel explicitly about early-phase drowsiness ([Kyle 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3900612/); standard delivery guidance, e.g., [UPenn sleep-restriction clinical outline](https://www.med.upenn.edu/cbti/assets/user-content/documents/btsd--advancedsleeprestriction-spielman.pdf)).
4. **Significant cognitive impairment.** Full CBT-I (digital or in-person) requires capacity to follow instructions and keep sleep diaries. Direct-to-patient education has been piloted in older adults with mild cognitive impairment ([BMC Geriatrics 2017, DOI 10.1186/s12877-017-0432-5](https://bmcgeriatr.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12877-017-0432-5)), but in dementia, taper with caregiver-supported sleep hygiene and specialist input is more appropriate — note that this is also the subgroup with the largest fracture harms (Table 1, row 3).
5. **Never stop abruptly.** Abrupt BZRA cessation after long-term use risks rebound insomnia, severe anxiety, and withdrawal seizures; tapering with monitoring is integral to the recommendation ([Pottie 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5951648/); [FDA boxed-warning update on benzodiazepine dependence/withdrawal, 2020](https://www.fda.gov/media/142368/download?attachment=)).
6. **Product-label and access limits for the digital option.** Somryst is labeled for adults 22+ with chronic insomnia ([clinician IFU, Nox Health 2025](https://noxmedical.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/Somryst-Clinician-Instructions-for-Use-2.0.pdf)); SleepioRx is cleared as an adjunct to usual care ([K233577](https://api.fda.gov/device/510k.json?search=device_name:Sleepio*&limit=5)). Both require internet/smartphone access and basic digital literacy; patients without it route to step 5(b) (nurse-delivered BBTI) or 5(c).
7. **Suspected sedative use disorder, dose escalation, or co-use with opioids/alcohol.** Manage with addiction-medicine involvement, not a primary-care fast taper; benzodiazepine–alcohol co-ingestion multiplies crash risk ~7.7-fold (pooled OR 7.69, 95% CI 4.33–13.65; [Dassanayake 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21247221/)) and the 2020 FDA boxed warning covers abuse, dependence, and combined opioid–benzodiazepine respiratory depression ([FDA 2020](https://www.fda.gov/media/142368/download?attachment=)).

## 4. Limitations & counter-evidence

1. **The core combination trials are small, old, and therapist-delivered.** Morin 2004 (N=76) and Baillargeon 2003 (N=65) date from two decades ago, used 8–10 weeks of face-to-face CBT-I in Canadian academic settings, and Morin's sample (mean age 62.5) is not strictly 65+. The pathway's central substitution — replacing that therapist with a prescription digital program or nurse-delivered BBTI — is an extrapolation: **no published RCT combining an FDA-cleared digital CBT-I product with a structured BZRA taper in adults 65+ was found** in this research (not found, searched 2026-06-11). YAWNS NB is the closest modern analogue, but Sleepwell is a self-help booklet package, not a cleared digital therapeutic.
2. **Discontinuation is a surrogate, not a harm outcome.** No deprescribing RCT has been powered to show reduced fractures, crashes, or dementia after stopping BZRAs; the harm-reversal logic chains observational effect sizes (Table 1) to trial discontinuation rates. Crowe & Stranks 2018 found cognitive deficits **persist after withdrawal**, so substitution mainly prevents further harm accrual rather than restoring function — and if some harm associations are confounded (next point), the avoided-harm benefit is smaller than Table 1 implies.
3. **Observational harm estimates carry confounding-by-indication and reverse-causation risk** (inherited from M1 Limitation 3). Insomnia itself raises fall risk; prodromal dementia causes sleep disturbance that gets treated with benzodiazepines (the Billioti de Gage OR 1.51 may partly reflect this — a live debate in the literature). The crash evidence is genuinely mixed in 65+: Dassanayake's elderly subgroup OR 1.13 (0.97–1.31) was not significant, against Hemmelgarn's significant RRs for long-half-life agents — Table 1 shows both deliberately.
4. **Withdrawal burden is real and underplayed by headline success rates.** In D-PRESCRIBE, 38% of patients attempting sedative-hypnotic taper reported withdrawal symptoms (none requiring hospitalization) ([Martin 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6248132/)); most trial "failures" (15–62% across arms) remain on the drug, and the best direct-to-patient package still leaves ~74% using at 6 months without the rest of the pathway behind it (100% − 26.2%, YAWNS NB Sleepwell arm).
5. **Almost all the pathway evidence is Canadian.** EMPOWER, D-PRESCRIBE, YAWNS NB, Morin, Baillargeon, and the Pottie guideline are all Canadian; US pharmacist scope of practice, pharmaceutical-opinion billing, and incentives differ by state, and the Medicare billing wrapper (M2 §2) has the unresolved G0552 contractor-pricing problem — the US economics of steps 2–3 are inferred, not demonstrated [speculative].
6. **The older-adult digital CBT-I trial is adjacent, not exact.** SHUTi OASIS enrolled ages 55–95 with mean ~66 and only 31% over 70; medication reduction was a secondary outcome; and SHUTi OASIS is a research adaptation — its relationship to the commercial Somryst product mirrors the trial-brand continuity caveat flagged in M2 Limitation 6.
7. **Selection into trials favors motivated patients.** EMPOWER/YAWNS NB participants were community-dwelling volunteers reachable by mail or pharmacy recruitment; frail, multimorbid, or cognitively impaired long-term users — the group with the largest absolute harms in Table 1 — are underrepresented, and Section 3's exclusions carve out exactly those patients (e.g., dementia). Population-level yield will be lower than trial percentages.
